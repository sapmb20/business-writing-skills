# The Science of Business Writing: the 8 S's (in full)

Part of `business-writing`, used at revision pass 3.

Based on Bill Birchard, "The Science of Strong Business Writing" (*Harvard Business Review*).
Make sure the writing has one or more of these characteristics. Not every piece needs all
eight. Pick the ones that serve the purpose and the reader.

1. **Simple.** Simplicity increases the brain's processing fluency.
   - Use short sentences, familiar words, and clean syntax. Minimize the brainpower readers need
     to understand the meaning.
   - Avoid long clauses, because they create room for error.
   - You are more persuasive when you strip overdressed ideas down to their essentials.
   - Cut extraneous words and use the active voice.
   - Drill down to what is truly salient and scrap tangential details.

2. **Specific.** Help the reader's brain process meaning more robustly.
   - Give readers a memorable shorthand phrase that helps them retain the message,
     such as Malcolm Gladwell's "tipping point."

3. **Surprising.** Surprise makes a message stick.
   - Readers appreciate unusual wordplay. For example, Biglari Holdings wrote in a shareholder
     letter: "Think of Biglari Holdings as a museum of businesses. Our preference is to collect masterpieces."

4. **Stirring.** Reason follows emotion. Readers feel words before they process them, and they
   combine the immediate feeling with the thought that follows to create meaning.
   - Readers don't miss emotionally charged words. "Outwit rivals" is more memorable than
     "challenge the competition."
   - Metaphors work well. Keep them short and snappy.
   - Keep it subtle: just enough to trigger the neural circuits for emotion, never melodrama.
   - Before composing, get your feelings straight as well as the facts. The goal is to make
     readers feel.

5. **Seductive.** People are wired to savor anticipation.
   - Put readers in a state of uncertainty so you can lead them to something better:
     - Start a report with a question.
     - Pose the customer's problem as a conundrum.
     - Position product development as solving a mystery.
   - Structure the piece deliberately to build curiosity about what's coming. Steve Jobs opened
     his 2005 Stanford commencement address: "I never graduated from college. Truth be told,
     this is the closest I've ever gotten to a college graduation. Today I want to tell you
     three stories from my life. That's it. No big deal. Just three stories."
   - Always deliver the payoff you promised.

6. **Smart.** Make readers feel smart. Give them an "aha" moment.
   - Deliver sudden pops of insight. They leave readers at ease, certain, and above all happy.
   - Draw fresh distinctions. Former IBM CEO Ginni Rometty: "It will not be a world of man
     versus machine; it will be a world of man plus machine."
   - Phrase a pragmatic message so that it also evokes a perennial, universal truth.

7. **Social.** People crave human connection, even in what they read. They don't just want to
   read about people; they want to understand what those people are thinking, quickly.
   - Reveal traces of the writer: voice, worldview, vocabulary, wit, syntax, rhythm, and
     sensibilities. When the assistant drafts for the user, these traces must be the user's, drawn
     from their own materials and prior writing, not the assistant's.
   - Include the human angle in any topic.
   - Use the second person ("you") to engage readers.

8. **Story-driven.** Stories, even fragments of them, engage large parts of the reader's brain,
   partly because they combine many of the S's above.
   - Where it fits, frame the message as a short narrative with a situation, an action, and a result.

**The golden rule the 8 S's lead to: reward readers as you would want to be rewarded yourself.**
