# Persuasion and logic (step 3, and revision pass 3)

Part of `business-writing`. Read this whenever a piece argues for something: a proposal, a
recommendation, an investment thesis, a request for money or people. Run it twice: once
while organizing, to build the argument, and once in revision, as a stress test.

Written for this repo. It draws on long-standing public ideas rather than any one text:
Aristotle's three appeals, Toulmin's claim-evidence-warrant model of an argument, and the
traditional list of informal fallacies.

## Build the argument

1. **State the claim in one sentence.** "We should move payroll to vendor B before Q3" is a
   claim. "Some thoughts on payroll" is not.
2. **Give the reasons, strongest first,** each backed by evidence the reader will accept:
   figures with their source, a precedent, a test result, a named expert. Business readers
   weigh numbers and track records more than adjectives.
3. **Make the link explicit.** Evidence supports a claim only through an assumption (Toulmin's
   *warrant*): "costs fell 12% at the pilot site, *and the pilot site is typical*, so costs
   will fall elsewhere." If the reader might reject the warrant, defend it.
4. **Concede the real downside, then answer it.** Naming the strongest objection before the
   reader does earns trust, and a recommendation that admits a cost reads as judged rather
   than sold. Answer it with a mitigation, a comparison ("higher fee, but less rework than
   today"), or a reason it matters less than the gain.
5. **Fit the appeal to the reader.** *Logos* (the evidence and the reasoning) carries most
   business writing. *Ethos* (why the writer is credible: role, record, sources) matters most
   to readers who don't know you. *Pathos* (what the reader cares about) works when it is
   specific and proportionate, such as a customer's lost week, never pressure or alarm.
6. **Ask for one specific action** with an owner and a date.

## Stress test: common fallacies

Reread the draft as a skeptical reader hunting for these.

| Fallacy | What it looks like | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hasty generalization | One client complained, so "clients are unhappy" | Give the count and the base rate, or narrow the claim |
| False cause (*post hoc*) | Sales rose after the redesign, so the redesign raised sales | Rule out the other changes (season, pricing, a competitor's exit), or say "coincided with" |
| False dilemma | "Either we cut 10% or we close the office" | Name the other options and say why they lose |
| Slippery slope | "If we allow one exception, every team will want one" | Show the mechanism, or drop the chain |
| Appeal to authority | "A top consultant said so" | Cite the evidence the authority relied on, and their expertise in *this* area |
| Bandwagon | "Every competitor is doing it" | Show why it fits *our* situation |
| Ad hominem | Dismissing a proposal because of who made it | Answer the argument |
| Straw man | Rebutting a weaker version of the other view | State the opposing view as its holder would, then answer that |
| Begging the question | "This is the best vendor because it is superior" | Supply an independent reason |
| Non sequitur | The conclusion doesn't follow from the reasons | Add the missing step, or change the conclusion |

## Tests for evidence

- **Relevant:** does it bear on *this* claim, or on a neighboring one?
- **Sufficient:** is there enough of it? One data point is an anecdote.
- **Representative:** does the sample match the population you generalize to?
- **Current and sourced:** is it recent enough for the decision, and can the reader trace it?
  Load `research-and-sources` to evaluate a source.
- **Consistent:** does any of your own evidence cut the other way? If so, address it before
  the reader finds it.
