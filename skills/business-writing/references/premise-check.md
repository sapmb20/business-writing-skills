# Verify the premise (step 1.5, in full)

Part of `business-writing`. The body of the skill carries the summary; this is the full rule set.

Most damaging errors in business writing are not errors of craft. They are confident
statements about the state of a relationship or a process that the writer never actually
confirmed: that an offer was made, that a decision was reached, that someone agreed, that
a deadline was extended.

Before drafting, list every claim about the relationship or the process that the piece
depends on, and mark each one:
- **Confirmed** — it exists in writing. Quote it, and use the quoted wording.
- **Inferred** — drawn from tone, a conversation, or what seemed implied.
- **Assumed** — you need it to be true and have no source at all.

**Choose the move; don't default to hedging.** Every claim that isn't Confirmed has five
available moves, and "I understood that..." is only one of them — usually the weakest,
because it costs warmth without buying confirmation and invites the reader to wonder whether
you understood correctly. In rough order of preference:
- **Cut it.** Ask first whether the claim is doing any work. Most unconfirmed claims are
  decorative, and the piece is shorter and stronger without them.
- **Ask.** Turn the claim into a direct question: "Could you confirm the start date?" This
  is the only move that actually resolves the uncertainty, and it reads as organized rather
  than unsure.
- **Anchor it.** State it as the plan you are acting on: "I'll hold the summer open from
  June 1." Presumptive but productive — it invites correction if it's wrong, and it reads as
  someone who manages their own commitments.
- **State it plainly.** Correct when the relationship is warm, the downside is low, and the
  reader can correct you in one line at no cost.
- **Hedge it.** Reserve for when you must reference the claim, cannot ask, and being wrong
  would be expensive.

**Calibrate on two things, not on caution.**
- **What the sentence does if the claim is false.** Merely inaccurate, and the reader
  shrugs? Low. Creates work or confusion? Medium. Reads as presumptuous, entitled, or
  accusatory — or forces the reader to correct you before they can say what they actually
  wrote to say? High, and it gets rewritten however likely you think it is.
- **What a correction costs the reader.** In a warm relationship with an open channel, a
  wrong claim is fixed in one line and nothing is lost. Across a seniority gap, with a
  stranger, or in a message that becomes a record, the correction is expensive or never
  comes, and the error stands as written.

Precision rises with the recipient count. A note to one friendly contact and the same note
copied to operations, HR, and compliance are not the same document, however warm the
original relationship.

**Don't over-correct into distance.** Hedging everything in a warm relationship is its own
failure: it reads as self-protective, it manufactures doubt where none existed, and between
people who like each other it lands as a small insult. Where warmth is high and the downside
is low, state things plainly and let the reader correct you.

**Four things stay absolute, at every level of warmth:**
- **Never attribute words to someone from memory.** The repair is to drop the attribution,
  not to hedge it: "I'm following up on the additional details" beats both "you said you
  would send details" and "I understood you would send details." The first accuses, the
  second sounds unsure, the third just asks. Quoting something they actually wrote is fine.
- **Never assert a commercial term** — compensation, dates, title, scope — that you were not
  given.
- **Never state as fact anything that would embarrass the user if it were quoted back.**
- **Never describe a stage of a process you cannot point to.**

The same discipline applies in reverse: when the reader has given you a fact, restate it so
that a misunderstanding surfaces now rather than later.
