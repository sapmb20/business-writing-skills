# Career Writing

Use this skill for résumés, cover letters, application and short-answer questions, LinkedIn
text, STAR interview stories, and offers. It feeds step 1 of `business-writing` (audience:
what the recruiter screens for) and step 2 (research: which proof points the user already has).

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and
`grammar-and-mechanics` (pass 7), before you deliver.

## When to Use

- Résumés, cover letters, LinkedIn text, and application or short-answer questions
- A one-page finance or banking résumé (`references/templates.md`)
- Preparing STAR interview stories
- Replying to, accepting, or declining an internship or job offer
- Interview preparation, thank-you notes, informational interviews, resignation letters

Run the `business-writing` sequence around it; this skill supplies the recruiting content.

**Job-search documents, in brief.**

- **Résumé:** contact line; education (with GPA when it helps); experience in reverse
  chronological order; leadership; skills and interests. One page for students and early
  career. For applicant tracking systems (ATS), use a single column, standard headings, no
  text boxes or tables, and the posting's own key terms where they are true of you.
- **Cover letter:** under a page, in three moves: the role and why this firm (something
  specific); two or three proof points that match the posting; the ask (an interview) and
  thanks. Address a named person when you can find one.
- **Thank-you note:** within 24 hours of an interview or coffee chat; one specific thing you
  learned or discussed, and a line on your fit.
- **Accepting or declining an offer:** see "Offers" below. Decline warmly and promptly; you
  may meet these people again.
- **Resignation:** brief and gracious: your last day, thanks, and an offer to help with the
  handover. Save complaints for the exit interview, if anywhere.

**Templates.** Read `references/templates.md` before drafting a finance résumé or cover
letter from scratch: a one-page résumé layout and a cover letter, each with its source and
license. A firm's or school's own template takes precedence where it differs, so ask
whether the user has one.

The structure and etiquette above set the frame; this skill's main job is **what fills the
bullets**: proof points, not labels.

## Match the skills to the career

Every career calls for a different mix of baseline skills. Emphasize the ones the target
reader screens for:
- **Finance and administration:** accuracy, organization, time management, Excel, and attention to detail
- **Sales and marketing:** persuasion, presentation, relationship-building, and writing
- **IT and engineering:** writing, customer service, problem-solving, and project management
- **Management:** supervision, leadership, research, negotiation, and strategic planning

---

## Replace skill labels with evidence

Never write an unsupported label such as "strong communicator." Replace it with a result.
- Label: "Strong communicator."
- Evidence: "Presented a valuation recommendation to 20 students and defended the underlying
  assumptions during Q&A."

This is the 8 S's "specific" and "story-driven" principles (`business-writing`) applied to a résumé: a concrete
action and result, told in one line.

### One proof point per core skill
Find, or help the user identify, at least one proof point for each of these skills:
- **Communication:** a presentation, client interaction, or team briefing
- **Writing:** an investment memo, research report, or executive summary
- **Organization:** managing deadlines, events, or several workstreams
- **Excel:** a financial model, dashboard, data analysis, or automated template
- **Leadership:** training, supervising, or coordinating others
- **Project management:** delivering a defined outcome under a deadline

Take proof points only from the user's own materials or answers. Never invent or inflate
numbers, titles, or outcomes. If a skill has no proof point, say so and suggest how the user
could earn one.

## STAR interview stories

Structure each story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Prepare stories that cover:
1. Solving an ambiguous problem
2. Catching an important error
3. Managing competing deadlines
4. Handling a difficult teammate or customer
5. Leading a project without formal authority

Keep the Action section about what the user personally did ("I," not "we"), and end the
Result section with a concrete outcome, quantified where the user can support the number.

**Use recordings or notes if the user has them.** If a coffee chat, informational
interview, or mock interview was recorded or noted, work from what was actually said: the
interviewer's own words about the role, and the user's own phrasing of what they did. That
beats reconstructing either from memory. Automatic meeting summaries can mix up who said
what, so check speaker roles against the transcript.

## Finance-role priorities

For finance applications, prioritize evidence of these capabilities:
1. **Concise business writing:** one-page company or investment memos
2. **Excel proficiency:** models that show formulas, data organization, sensitivity analysis,
   and error checks
3. **Attention to detail:** model checks and document-review checklists
4. **Customer orientation:** stakeholder communication treated as a core finance skill,
   especially for banking, wealth management, and client-facing roles
5. **Presentation:** explaining a recommendation and defending its assumptions
6. **Project ownership:** coordinating people, deadlines, and deliverables, not only
   completing assigned analysis

## Offers and offer threads

An offer is a commercial agreement that happens to arrive by email. Warmth is not the
deliverable; a clear record is. Apply `email-writing` and its thread-reply rules to the thread itself, and these
rules to its content.

### Accept, decline, or ask — in words that cannot be read two ways

Gratitude is not acceptance. "I am very grateful for the opportunity" and "I look forward
to receiving the details" leave both sides free to believe different things about whether
you have a job.

- **Accept:** "I'm delighted to accept the Summer 2027 internship at Dimension Capital."
  Use the verb *accept*, name the role, and name the organization.
- **Decline:** "After careful thought, I'm declining the offer." Use the verb, give one
  sentence of reason, thank them, and leave the door open.
- **Not ready:** say so and put a date on it: "May I confirm by October 2? I have one
  process outstanding and want to give you a clean answer."

Never let an offer sit in a state where a reader would have to guess.

### Get the terms in writing before treating an offer as real

A verbal or informal offer is a good sign, not a commitment. Ask for these, as a short list,
in the reply that accepts:

1. Exact start and end dates
2. Location, and whether the role is in person, hybrid, or remote
3. Compensation, and whether housing or a stipend is included
4. Team or desk, and who you will report to
5. Paperwork: offer letter, background check, work authorization, school forms
6. The name of the person who owns the next step

Asking is not pushy. Every one of these is something the employer already knows and will
have to tell you eventually, and asking early is what someone who manages a process looks
like.

### Offers far in the future

Internship offers often land six to twelve months before the start date, sometimes with
nothing but an email thread to show for it. Long gaps are where offers quietly evaporate —
budgets change, the sponsor leaves, the program is restructured.

- Keep the whole thread. It is the only record of what was agreed.
- If no written offer letter follows within two or three weeks, ask for one directly.
- Re-confirm on a schedule — roughly quarterly, and again six to eight weeks before the
  start date — with a short note that restates the dates and asks what you should be doing
  to prepare. Each note is also a reason to stay visible to the team.
- Every time you accept an undated promise of "more details soon," you have agreed to wait
  indefinitely. Put your own date on it (the date-anchor rule in `email-writing`).
