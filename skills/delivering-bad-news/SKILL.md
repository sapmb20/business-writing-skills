# Delivering Bad News

Bad news changes how a message should be organized. Most business messages lead with the
main point. Bad news often shouldn't, and when it does lead, it must arrive carrying
something the reader can use. This skill picks the pattern and supplies its parts.

Sources: "Writing a Bad-News Letter" (tulsaccprof, YouTube) and "How to Give Your Boss Bad
News" (Alexa Fischer, YouTube). The two sequences and their parts come from those videos.
The customer-facing sequence agrees with the open textbook *Communication at Work*
(Rivers, eCampusOntario, CC BY 4.0, section 8.3), which the templates credit element by
element.
Section 4 covers the standard bad-news correspondence types (see ATTRIBUTION.md at the repo
root). The pattern-choice table, the override signals, the common failures, and "don't
wait" are this skill's own synthesis.

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and
`grammar-and-mechanics` (pass 7), before you deliver.

## When to Use

- Denying, declining, or rejecting something: a request, an application, a claim, a refund
- Announcing an unwelcome change: a price increase, a cut, a delay, a policy change
- Reporting a missed target, a mistake, or a problem to a manager or client
- Reviewing a draft that delivers bad news and reads as blunt, evasive, or rambling

This skill is step 3 (organization) of `business-writing` for these messages. Run the rest
of that sequence around it: purpose and audience first, the premise check before stating
what went wrong, and revision afterwards.

## 1. Choose the pattern

Decide by who the reader is and what the message has to protect.

| | Indirect pattern | Upward pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reader** | Customers, clients, employees receiving a decision | Your boss, or anyone who owns the problem with you |
| **What it protects** | The relationship, when the reader is disappointed | The reader's time and trust, when they need to act |
| **Where the news goes** | After a neutral opening and the reasons | Early, after one line on the goal and a short account |
| **What follows the news** | A positive alternative, then goodwill | One to three solutions |
| **Typical medium** | Letter or email | In person or a call (see `selecting-medium`) |

Two signals override the default:
- **The reader must act fast, or will be hurt by delay** (a safety issue, a deadline, a
  legal consequence): go direct with any reader. A buffer that delays urgent news is an
  evasion.
- **The news is routine or minor for this reader** (a small back-order, an unchanged
  schedule): go direct, with a brief reason. The full indirect sequence would make a small
  thing sound large.

When unsure, ask one question: is this reader deciding what to do next, or absorbing a
decision already made? Deciding → upward pattern. Absorbing → indirect pattern.

## 2. The indirect pattern (customers and employees)

The goal is to keep the reader satisfied with the relationship even though they are
disappointed by the news. Five parts, in this order:

1. **Neutral opening.** Start with something relevant, true, and neutral: thanks for the
   request, agreement on a shared goal, the context of the decision. Don't blurt out the
   news, and don't hint at a yes the rest of the message will take back.
2. **Reasons.** Explain why, before stating the decision, so the reader meets the logic
   first. Choose reasons that matter to the reader, not internal detail.
3. **The bad news.** State it clearly, once. Place it after the reasons, often in the middle
   of a paragraph rather than alone at the start of one. Clear, not buried: the reader must
   not finish the message unsure what the answer was.
4. **Cushion.** Pair the news with a positive alternative: what you can do, another option,
   a partial yes, a next step open to them.
5. **Goodwill close.** End forward-looking and warm, in a way that keeps the relationship.
   Don't restate the bad news, and don't apologize again.

**Common failures:**
- A buffer so long or so upbeat that the reader expects good news.
- Reasons that hide behind "policy" without saying what the policy protects.
- The news implied but never stated, so the reader writes back to ask.
- An alternative that isn't real or isn't useful to this reader.
- A close that invites the reader to reopen a final decision ("Please don't hesitate to
  contact me if you disagree").

## 3. The upward pattern (telling your boss)

Bad news upward has a high "squirm factor" (Fischer). Discomfort makes the messenger
ramble, and the rambling spreads the anxiety to the listener, who wants to know what is
going on as soon as possible. Plan it as four parts:

1. **Set the stage.** In a sentence, recap the objective the news is measured against:
   "We set out to reach X this quarter; here's where we are."
2. **The plot.** Say what happened, short. This is where people ramble through who did
   what, and where the boss stops listening. Give only the causes the decision needs.
3. **The bad news.** Say clearly what is wrong and why it matters. Get to the point.
4. **The solution.** The part most people forget, and the best chance to stand out.
   Arrive with a proposed fix, ideally one to three options with a recommendation. Options
   turn the bad news into a decision the boss can make, and show leadership instead of
   dependence.

**Before the conversation:** map the story, know the key points and numbers, and have the
solutions ready. Don't work them out in the room.

**Don't wait.** Bad news gets worse with age, and a boss who hears it late from someone
else loses trust in the messenger.

Check each claim against step 1.5 of `business-writing` before you go in. Say what you know
happened, not what you suspect, and never assign blame from memory.

## 4. Bad-news correspondence types

Read `references/templates.md` for fill-in templates of an indirect bad-news letter and a
complaint response (adjustment), each with its source and license.

Many business-writing texts state the indirect sequence of section 2 in four parts: context
(the old "buffer"), explanation, the news, goodwill. Readers settle on an attitude early in
a message, which is why high-stakes bad news goes indirect. When stakes are low and the
reasons don't matter much, a direct no (the answer, a reason, goodwill) is fine.

| Type | What it is | How to write it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Refusal** | Saying no to a request, a proposal, an invitation, or a claim | **Low stakes** (declining an invitation): short and gracious. **High stakes:** the indirect pattern. Open with genuine appreciation and no false hope; give the facts from the reader's side, specific enough that the no follows naturally; state the no once, plainly, without apologizing for it; offer a real alternative if one exists. Don't close by apologizing again |
| **Adjustment** | Your answer to a complaint: what you will do about it | Stay courteous however the complaint was worded; focus on the fix, not the fault; settle quickly and give reasonable benefit of the doubt. **Full adjustment** (your error): confirm the facts and the policy, then **lead with the yes** ("Your replacement ships today"). Explain the cause only if it restores confidence, and close looking ahead. Choose words carefully, since an admission such as calling a product "faulty" can carry legal weight; ask counsel when unsure. **Partial adjustment** (the claim is only partly valid): **explain first**, then say what you will grant, and point out the customer's part tactfully |
| **Complaint** (you are writing it) | Asking an organization to fix a problem | Calm and specific; anger is easy to dismiss, and a public post before a private request reads as an attack. Give **(1)** the item, order or invoice number, and dates; **(2)** what went wrong, as facts, without guessing at causes; **(3)** the remedy you want; **(4)** copies (never originals) of receipts, contracts, or photos. Use the company's complaint channel if it has one; otherwise customer service at a large firm, or the owner or sales head at a small one. Escalate if there's no reply |
| **Collection** | Getting an overdue bill paid while keeping the customer | A series that grows firmer and stays courteous. **First:** a friendly reminder. **Then:** a direct request for payment that asks whether something is in the way and makes paying easy. **Last:** an explicit demand that notes how patient you have been and names the next step (an agency or legal action). In the US, third-party debt collectors must follow the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act; when in doubt, get legal advice |

**A short internal notice is bad news too.** An announcement in the passive voice ("It has
been decided that...") sounds imposed and hides who decided. Lead with the reason instead,
as `memo-writing` shows.

Declining a **job offer** follows `career-writing`'s offer section.

## 5. Easy to confuse

- **Indirect vs. upward:** the indirect pattern delays the news behind a neutral opening and
  reasons to protect a relationship. The upward pattern states the news after a one-line
  recap and a short account, because the reader needs to act.
- **Alternative vs. solution:** an alternative gives the reader something positive in place
  of what they lost. A solution fixes or contains the problem itself.
- **Neutral opening vs. setting the stage:** both come first. A neutral opening is a buffer
  that doesn't signal the outcome; setting the stage is a factual recap of the goal.
- **Reasons vs. rambling:** reasons are chosen so the reader accepts the decision. Rambling is
  a chronological account that serves the writer's discomfort.
- **Refusal vs. adjustment:** a refusal says no to a request. An adjustment answers a complaint
  and usually says yes, fully or in part. Only a partial adjustment puts the explanation first.
- **Bad news vs. "main point first":** leading with the point (`email-writing`) is the default
  for requests and routine messages. Bad news to customers and employees is the main
  exception. Bad news to a boss keeps the point early.

## 6. Delivering the draft or plan

- Name the pattern you used and why, in one line.
- For the indirect pattern, label the five parts in your notes to the user (not in the
  draft), so they can check that the news is stated and the alternative is real.
- For upward news, give a talking-points outline rather than a script: the one-line goal,
  what happened in two or three bullets, the news in one sentence, and the options with a
  recommendation. If an email must follow, it records the decision reached, not the
  squirming that preceded it.
- Flag any cause or blame the user hasn't confirmed.
