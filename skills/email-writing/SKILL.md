# Email Writing

Use this skill whenever the medium is email (step 1.4 of `business-writing`, confirmed by
`selecting-medium`). It covers the parts of an email that the general sequence doesn't: the
subject line, the greeting, the opening, the request, the close, and the signature. These
are standard business conventions. Apply them together with the 8 S's in `business-writing`
and the `line-editing` skill.

When the email is a reply inside an existing thread, also read `references/threads.md`,
starting with its parse checklist.

Read `references/templates.md` when drafting from a blank page: a request email, a
follow-up, and a full-block business letter, each with its source and license.

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and
`grammar-and-mechanics` (pass 7), before you deliver.

## When to Use

- Drafting any professional email: outreach, requests, thank-yous, follow-ups
- Replying inside an existing thread (load `references/threads.md`)
- Reviewing an email draft before it is sent
- Inquiries, acknowledgments, cover emails for attachments, reference letters, and formal
  business letters (section 9)
- Sales emails and e-newsletters: follow this skill for the email conventions, and
  `promotional-writing` for the selling and the opt-out rules

For anything with real stakes, run the full `business-writing` sequence first. Its premise
check (step 1.5) and context gate (step 1.6) catch the failures these conventions don't.

## 1. Before drafting: one email, one purpose

- Give each email a single primary purpose, usually one request or one piece of news. If
  there are two unrelated requests, send two emails.
- **One thread per topic.** If a thread on this topic already exists, write into it
  instead of starting a new email for each follow-up idea or question. A new email strips
  the context of the original thread and clutters the reader's inbox, and everyone should
  be working from the same record (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 2).
- Decide what the reader should **do** after reading (reply with a time, approve a
  budget, review an attachment). Everything else in the email supports that action.
- Most readers see email on a phone first. Aim for a body that fits on one phone screen:
  about 75 to 200 words for a request. Longer content belongs in an attachment or a meeting.

---

## 2. Subject line

The subject line decides whether the email gets opened and how fast it gets found later.
- State the topic **and** the request: "Request: 20-minute call about corporate finance
  internships," not "Quick question" or "Hello."
- **Put the call to action in the subject line** when the email asks for something. Go past
  "Action required": name what the reader must do and, when it fits, how long it takes —
  "5-minute survey feedback for Project X." When a time estimate doesn't fit, name the
  action and its owner: "Elon to approve Q4 spending estimates." The reader should know
  what is expected before opening the email (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 1).
- In warm outreach, put the connection in the subject line so the reader recognizes it:
  "Following up from [mutual contact]'s introduction."
- Keep it to about 6 to 10 words so it isn't cut off on a phone.
- Use a prefix when the reader's inbox needs one: "Action needed:", "FYI:", "Due Fri:". A
  prefix alone only says an action exists; the words after it must say which one.
- If the topic of a thread changes, change the subject line.

---

## 3. Greeting

Match the formality to the relationship and to the reader's seniority. When unsure, start
more formal and mirror the reader's reply.
- **Student or junior writer to a professional they barely know:** "Dear Mr. Moreno," or
  "Dear Ms. Chen,". If the reader's title or pronouns are unknown, use the full name:
  "Dear Jordan Lee,". Don't guess a gendered title from a name.
- **Colleagues and people who have used your first name:** "Hi Daniel," or "Hello Daniel,".
- **Groups:** "Hi team," or "Hello all,". Avoid "Dear Sir or Madam" and "To whom it may
  concern" whenever a name can be found.
- Spell the name exactly as the reader spells it. Check it against their signature or LinkedIn.

---

## 4. Opening

The first two lines show in the inbox preview. Use them to answer "who is this and why
are they writing?"
- **Put the bottom line up front.** Give the reason for writing in the first or second
  sentence. When the email asks for something, the ask comes first and the background
  second, under a label such as "Context:" that the reader can choose to skip.
- **Exception: bad news.** A denial, rejection, or unwelcome change sent to customers or
  employees uses the indirect pattern instead: a neutral opening, then the reasons, then the
  news. Bad news to your boss keeps the point early but arrives with solutions. Load
  `delivering-bad-news`.
- **Don't justify before asking, especially upward.** Writing to someone senior, the urge
  to explain yourself first so you don't seem to be bothering them backfires. A senior
  reader wants to know what you need and how they can help, then move on (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 4).
- **Remind the reader of the connection** in the first sentence when they may not
  remember you: who introduced you, where you met, and when.
- **Cut filler openers** such as "I hope this email finds you well," "I know you're busy,"
  and "Sorry to bother you." They delay the point, and the apologies make the writer sound
  unsure (see weasel words and negatives in `line-editing`).

---

## 5. Body

- Keep paragraphs to 1 to 3 sentences, with a blank line between them.
- Give only the context the reader needs to act. A sentence or two of who you are is
  enough for outreach. Save the full story for the meeting or the attachment.
- Use a list for several questions, options, or action items, with a complete-sentence
  lead-in ending in a colon (`lists-and-headings`). Don't use headings in a short email.
- Use bold sparingly, only for a deadline or a decision the reader can't miss.
- **Hyperlink, don't paste URLs.** Link descriptive text (Cmd+K on Mac, Ctrl+K on Windows)
  instead of pasting a raw URL. It reads cleaner, and a retyped or trimmed URL can't break
  (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 6). In plain-text drafts handed to the user, write
  `[link text](URL)` and tell them to convert it.
- Make the ask actionable in the body: the date range, the format, the file type you need.

---

## 6. The request

Make it easy for the reader to say yes.
- **Make the request explicit.** Phrase it as a direct question: "Would you have 20
  minutes for a video call in the next two weeks?"
- **Make it specific:** length, format (call, video, coffee), and a time frame.
- **Give options:** two or three time windows, plus an offer to work around the reader's schedule.
- **Take on the logistics yourself:** "I'll send a calendar invite and a Zoom link."
- **Keep the favor small.** For a first request to someone busy, ask for 15 to 20 minutes, not an hour.

---

## 7. Close and signature

- End with the next step or a short thank-you, not both at length. "Thank you again for
  the offer. I'll keep the call to 20 minutes and come prepared." commits to something and
  respects the reader's time.
- When the other side owes you something undated, close on a date you commit to:
  "If I haven't heard by October 2, I'll follow up then." Don't end on "let me know if you
  need anything else from me," which gives you no reason to write again
  (`references/threads.md`).
- Use a standard sign-off: "Best regards," "Best," or "Thank you,". Avoid "Cheers" and
  emoji in first contact.
- The signature block should hold the full name, role or affiliation (school, graduation
  year, and program for students), and a phone number. A LinkedIn URL is optional.

## Networking and follow-up

- **Timing:** reach out within one to two weeks of meeting someone, while they still
  remember you.
- **CC:** copy someone only when they need to see the email. Don't copy the person who
  introduced you unless they made a formal email introduction; if they did, reply-all once
  and move them to BCC with a note.
- **One request, one owner.** When several people are on the To or CC line, each tends to
  assume someone else will answer. If you need a response, address the request to one named
  person, or send it to that person alone.
- **No reply:** send one polite follow-up after about a week, in the same thread, with the
  request restated in one line. Don't send a third message.
- **After the meeting:** send a thank-you within 24 hours. Mention one specific piece of
  advice and what you'll do with it.

## Following up on an undecided process

A status follow-up is not a networking follow-up. You are writing while someone else holds
a decision about you — an application, an offer, a proposal, an invoice, a review. The
governing rule is that **you may not write the outcome you want into the email.**

- **Describe the stage you can prove, not the one you hope for.** "Thank you for the time
  on Tuesday and for walking me through the internship" is safe at any stage. "Thank you
  again for the opportunity to join the firm" asserts an offer, and if no offer exists it
  forces the reader to correct you before answering you — which is how a polite email
  becomes evidence that you don't track your own affairs.
- **Drop the attribution from the commitment you are chasing.** Not "you said you would
  send it," and not the hedged "I understood that you would" either — just "I'm following
  up on the additional details you mentioned." Don't make a claim about what the reader
  promised in order to ask them for it. See step 1.5 of `business-writing`.
- **Restate your interest once, plainly,** and don't argue your case again. The decision is
  not being made in this thread.
- **Ask a bounded question.** "Is there anything further you need from me, and when should
  I expect to hear about next steps?" beats "any updates?" — it gives the reader something
  answerable even when the decision isn't made.
- **Offer something if you have it:** a transcript, a reference, a writing sample, availability.
- **Cadence:** one follow-up after about a week of silence, a second only if the reader gave
  you a date that has now passed, and reference that date when you do.

## 8. Final checks before sending

- **Draft before addressing.** For anything going to a superior or an outsider,
  fill in the To line last, so a half-finished draft can't be sent. Write the subject line
  last, so it matches what the message became.
- Email is still the right medium. Very sensitive content, a misunderstanding, or a problem
  that needs back-and-forth may belong in a call (`selecting-medium`). Every email can be
  forwarded and is subject to legal disclosure.
- The reader's name is spelled correctly, in the greeting and everywhere else.
- Every recipient on the To line is greeted, and anyone newly added is acknowledged by name.
- Every status this email asserts is one you could quote from writing. Nothing you merely
  inferred appears as a fact.
- Nothing is attributed to the reader as their own words unless you would bet on the wording.
- Every hard fact you were given — dates, numbers, locations — is mirrored back.
- Every question the reader asked you is answered.
- There is a date in the email: theirs, or one you commit to.
- The subject line matches the final content.
- Any attachment mentioned is actually attached, and every link works and sits on
  descriptive text, not a raw URL.
- The subject line names the action (and its owner or time) whenever the email asks for one.
- Any recipient added or removed since the last message is announced in the first line
  (`references/threads.md`).
- Reply-all is deliberate: every person on the list needs this message.
- Times include a time zone when the reader may be in a different one.
- The request can be found in under five seconds of skimming.
- The signature block is complete and identical to the one you used earlier in the thread.

## 9. Common correspondence types

| Type | What it must do |
| --- | --- |
| **Inquiry** | Ask one clear question or a short numbered set, say why you're asking and by when you need it, and make answering easy |
| **Response to an inquiry** | Answer the question first, then add what helps; if you can't answer, say who can |
| **Acknowledgment** | Confirm receipt, say what happens next and when |
| **Cover message (transmittal)** | Name what is attached and why, point to anything that needs the reader's attention, and state the action and date |
| **Reference letter** | Only for someone you can recommend honestly: how you know them and for how long, two or three specific strengths with evidence, and a clear level of endorsement |
| **Formal business letter** | Full-block format on letterhead, for external, official, or legal matters (`references/templates.md`) |

Bad-news versions of any of these (a refusal, a complaint response) follow
`delivering-bad-news`.

## Set up the email client once

Two settings contain the damage of a rushed send (HBR × Jeff Su, tips 7 and 8). Recommend
them when the user is setting up an email workflow, or after a send mistake:

- **Default reply behavior: Reply, not Reply all.** A mistake made in a hurry then reaches
  one person instead of the whole list. Reply-all becomes a deliberate choice. In Gmail:
  Settings → General → Default reply behavior.
- **Undo send: 30 seconds.** Mistakes surface seconds after sending, usually on a reread
  from the reader's side, and the common default window is 5 seconds. In Gmail: Settings →
  General → Undo Send → Send cancellation period.

Changing a user's account settings is their call. Recommend these; change them only when
the user asks.

## Delivering an email draft

Give the subject line first, then the body, then the signature block. Put any facts the
user must fill in (names, phone number, dates) in [brackets] and list them after the draft.
