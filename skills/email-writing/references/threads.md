# Email Threads and Replies

Use this file whenever you are writing **into an existing thread** rather than starting one:
replying to news, answering a question, or continuing a negotiation. The `email-writing`
skill covers the parts of an email; this file covers what a reply owes the message above it.

Most reply failures are not writing failures. They happen before the first word: the writer
skims the inbound message, misses what it actually committed to, misses who it added to the
thread, and answers the feeling instead of the content.

---

## 1. Parse the inbound message before drafting

Work through the message you are answering and write down, for yourself:

- **Decisions.** What was actually decided, in their words? Quote it.
- **Commitments.** What did they promise to do, and **does any of it carry a date?** A
  commitment without a date ("soon," "in the coming days," "shortly") is an open loop, not
  a plan. Treat it as the main thing your reply must close.
- **Questions asked of you.** Every one gets an answer, including the soft ones. An open
  offer to answer questions expires quickly, so take it up in this reply.
- **Facts stated in passing.** Dates, amounts, locations, names, titles. A fact dropped in
  a parenthesis — "start date (June)" — is still the only version of that fact on the record.
- **Recipients added or removed.** See below.
- **What is still missing.** List the things you would need to act, that nobody has written
  down yet.

Keep what you extract. Organize incoming information as it arrives, so the thread's state
never lives only in your memory.

**If the inbound message is disorganized,** group its themes and open your reply with a
summary of the sender's main points in two or three sentences, then respond. It confirms
you understood before you act on a misreading, and it helps the sender organize their own
thinking (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 5). Skip it for a message that is already clear; summarizing a
tidy email reads as padding.

Anything in the last two categories is the raw material for your reply. If your draft
doesn't touch them, you are writing a receipt, not a reply.

---

## 2. The recipient delta

Compare the recipient list of the message you received against the one you sent.

- **You are adding or removing someone.** Say so in one line at the very top, set apart
  from the body in parentheses and italics, with the reason: *(Adding Amy for the
  paperwork; moving Julian to BCC to spare his inbox.)* Readers see the change before
  anything else (HBR × Jeff Su, tip 3).
- **Someone was added.** Greet them and acknowledge them by name: "Hi Julian — and nice to
  meet you, Alejandro and Amy." Never address three people with a greeting to one.
- **Ask what the addition means.** People are added to threads for a reason, usually because
  the work is moving to them. Decide explicitly whether logistics now route through the new
  party, and if it is unclear, ask in one line: "Should I send the paperwork to Amy directly?"
- **Their first impression is this email.** A new reader has no history with you. A reply
  that says only "thank you, this is great news" is the entire basis on which they will
  judge you.
- **Someone was removed.** Leave them off. Don't re-add a person the sender dropped.
- Reply-all only when everyone on the list needs the content. Otherwise reply to the sender
  and say why you narrowed it.

---

## 3. What a substantive reply must contain

In this order, as briefly as each allows:

1. **Confirm the decision in your own words,** so the record shows you understood it the
   same way they do.
2. **Mirror back every hard fact** you were given — dates, numbers, locations, names. This
   is the cheapest error check in business correspondence: if their June is your June, it
   costs one clause; if it isn't, you find out now instead of in May.
3. **Answer every question they asked.**
4. **Ask yours,** grouped as a short list, framed around what you need in order to act.
5. **Name the next step and who owns it.**

---

## 4. Never close an exchange without a date anchor

An exchange that ends with "let me know if you need anything else from me" or "I look
forward to the details" hands control to the other side and gives you no reason to write
again. You will either wait indefinitely or send a follow-up that has to manufacture a
pretext.

Instead, put a date in the last line and commit to it:

> If I haven't heard by October 2, I'll follow up then so it doesn't slip.

This does three things: it converts their open loop into a scheduled one, it pre-authorizes
your next email so it needs no excuse, and it tells the reader you track your commitments.
Then honor the date — it is now a promise you made in writing.

---

## 5. Gratitude is a clause, not a message

Warmth is necessary and cheap. It is not content.

- A reply whose entire body is thanks and enthusiasm spends a turn in the conversation and
  advances nothing.
- Exclamation points do not substitute for confirming a fact.
- The strongest thank-you commits to something: "Thank you — I'm holding the summer open and
  won't commit elsewhere."

## 6. Worked example: an internship offer thread

**The inbound message.** A hiring contact writes to say the writer has been selected, adds
two colleagues to the thread, mentions "more details soon on start date (June) & logistics,"
and closes with "if you have any questions, don't hesitate to reach out."

Parsed: one decision (selected); one undated commitment (details "soon"); one open invitation
(questions); one passing fact (June); two new recipients; and missing — exact dates, location,
in-person or remote, compensation, paperwork, and who owns the next step.

**The weak reply:**

> Hello Julian,
>
> That is fantastic news! I am very grateful for the opportunity.
>
> I look forward to receiving the additional details. In the meantime, please let me know if
> you need anything else from me.

It greets one of three readers, accepts nothing, verifies nothing, asks nothing, declines the
invitation to ask, and re-accepts the same undated promise that already required a chase.

**The strong reply:**

> Hi Julian — and nice to meet you, Alejandro and Amy.
>
> Thank you. I'm delighted to accept the Summer 2027 internship at Dimension Capital.
>
> Noted that the start is in June — I'll hold my summer open and won't commit elsewhere.
> Whenever convenient, three things would help me plan:
>
> - the exact start and end dates,
> - whether the role is in person in Coral Gables, and
> - any paperwork or background check you'd need from me.
>
> If I haven't heard by October 2, I'll follow up then so it doesn't slip.

Same length, same warmth. It greets everyone, accepts unambiguously, mirrors the one fact on
the record, uses the door the sender opened, and ends on a date.
