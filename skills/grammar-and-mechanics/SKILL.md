# Grammar and Mechanics

Errors distract, irritate, or mislead readers, and they cost the writer credibility.
Proofreading is a sign of respect for the reader and of professionalism. Spell checkers and
grammar checkers help, but they also make writers overconfident. A typo that forms a real
word (*coarse* for *course*, *too* for *to*) gets through.

Sources: standard English usage as set out in the major style guides (see ATTRIBUTION.md at
the repo root). The finance-number conventions in §6 and the finance additions to
capitalization are this skill's own, labeled as such.

## When to Use

- The final pass on any draft (`business-writing` revision pass 7)
- Copyediting someone else's text
- A specific question: comma or semicolon, *that* or *which*, *who* or *whom*, how to write a
  number, a date, or an abbreviation
- Checking every number, date, and unit in a financial or quantitative document
- Deciding between two easily confused words

**Where it sits in the family.** This is mechanics. Run it *after* `line-editing` (wordiness,
voice, parallelism) and `lists-and-headings` (format). A sentence you're about to cut doesn't
need its commas fixed first. An organizational or professional style guide overrides these
defaults; when one applies, follow it.

## 1. Proofread in stages

Proofread in several passes, whoever wrote the text. Each pass looks for one class of problem.
Adapt the stages to the document and to your own weak spots.

1. **First stage: format and consistency.** The format is right for the document type. Style is
   consistent across headings, terminology, spacing, and fonts. Figures and tables are numbered
   correctly.
2. **Second stage: the line.** Check:
   - grammar and usage (§2-3) and punctuation (§4);
   - abbreviations and capitalization, which must be correct and consistent (§5);
   - spelling, including **names and places**;
   - web, email, and other addresses;
   - **every figure in a table, chart, or list**, against its source;
   - **editing debris**: words or numbers left behind when text was moved or cut.
3. **Final stage: the whole.** Recheck the goals (does it still serve this audience and
   purpose?), the appearance, and, for a crucial document, a review by a trusted colleague.

Proofreading often turns up a passage that needs revising, not just correcting. Send it back to
revision.

## 2. Sentence faults

| Fault | What it is | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Comma splice** | Two independent clauses joined only by a comma ("The audit ran late, we missed the filing.") | A semicolon; a semicolon plus a conjunctive adverb and comma ("; therefore,"); a comma plus a coordinating conjunction; two sentences; or subordinate one clause ("Because the audit ran late, we missed the filing.") |
| **Run-on (fused) sentence** | Two sentences with no punctuation between them | The same fixes as a comma splice |
| **Fragment** | Punctuated as a sentence but missing a subject or finite verb, or only a subordinate clause or phrase | Attach it to a neighbor or supply the missing part. Watch openers like *which*, *because*, *although*, *such as*, *for example*, and verbals (*Providing...*, *To work...*), which can't serve as the verb |
| **Dangling modifier** | An opening (or closing) phrase with no logical subject ("Reviewing the model, the formula error was obvious.") | Put the doer into the phrase ("When I reviewed the model, ...") or into the main clause ("...I spotted the formula error") |
| **Misplaced modifier** | The modifier attaches to the wrong word ("She only checked the summary" ≠ "She checked only the summary.") | Move it next to what it modifies. *Only*, *almost*, *just*, and *even* need special care |
| **Squinting modifier** | Can attach to either neighbor ("Analysts who revise forecasts often lose credibility.") | Move it or rewrite so only one reading survives |
| **Stacked modifiers** | A pileup before the noun ("customer retention incentive program review") | Unpack with prepositional phrases ("review of the incentive program for retaining customers") |
| **Faulty subordination** | The main idea sits in the subordinate clause, or a long subordinate clause buries it | Put the main point in the main clause, and put that clause first if the subordinate one is long |
| **Missing subject or verb** | The writer assumed a word ("Will send the figures Monday.") | Supply it ("I will send the figures Monday.") |
| **Rambling sentence** | More than the reader can absorb | Split it, with the main message in the first sentence |
| **Faulty predication** | The predicate says something illogical about the subject ("Her job is an analyst.") | "She is an analyst." |
| **Mixed construction** | The parts don't fit grammatically or logically ("Because the server failed explains the delay.") | Rebuild around a real subject |
| **Garbled sentence** | Too tangled to patch | Don't patch it. List its ideas, order them logically, and write new sentences |

## 3. Agreement, case, reference, tense, mood

Read `references/usage.md` for the marks and mechanics this page summarizes (every
punctuation mark, numbers, abbreviations, capitalization, italics) and more confused-word
pairs.

**Subject-verb agreement:**

- The verb agrees with the **subject**, not with a nearby noun: "One in ten invoices *is*
  late", "Each of the managers *supervises*." The same holds for an inverted sentence, and
  for a complement ("The topic *is* employee benefits").
- *One*, *each*, *type*, *part*, *series*, and *portion* take a singular verb ("A series of
  meetings *was* held").
- *Some*, *none*, *all*, *more*, and *most* follow their noun: singular with a mass noun,
  plural with a count noun.
- **Measurements and totals** are a unit: "Six months *is* the usual lockup period."
- *The number* is singular; *a number* is plural.
- Words like *news* and *economics* are singular. So is a title ("*The Intelligent Investor* is").
- **Collective nouns** (committee, board, team) are singular when acting as a unit. To stress
  the individuals, write "committee members."
- With *who* or *that*, the verb agrees with the antecedent: "one of the reports that
  *require*..."
- **Compound subjects:** *and* is plural, unless the parts are one thing ("My mentor and
  manager *is*..."). With *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the
  **nearer** part. *Each* or *every* before a compound makes it singular.

**Pronouns:**

- **Antecedents:** every pronoun needs a clear, nearby antecedent. Avoid a bare *this*, a
  hidden antecedent, and a name repeated in parentheses after the pronoun (rewrite instead).
- **Agreement:** pronouns agree in number and gender. Avoid the generic *he* and gender
  stereotypes ("the nurse... she"). Use a plural antecedent, singular *they* (accepted by
  APA and Chicago), or rewrite to drop the pronoun.
- ***This kind / these kinds*:** keep the demonstrative, the noun, and the object of *of*
  agreeing.

**Case:**

- Subjective after a linking verb, and after *than* or *as* ("earns more than I [do]").
- Objective after an action verb or a preposition ("between you and **me**", "invited Priya and
  **me**").
- Test a compound by dropping the other noun ("*We* analysts review" → "*We* review"). An appositive takes
  the case of the noun it renames.
- Use the possessive before a gerund ("objected to **our** leaving early").
- *Its*, *whose*, and *theirs* never take an apostrophe.

**Restrictive vs. nonrestrictive:**

- A **nonrestrictive** element adds information that can be removed. Set it off with commas and
  introduce it with ***which*** ("The 10-K, which was filed Tuesday, shows...").
- A **restrictive** element limits the meaning. It takes no commas and is introduced with
  ***that*** ("Firms that hedge currency risk report steadier margins").
- One comma can change the meaning. "The bonus went to the analysts who stayed late" (only
  some) and "The bonus went to the analysts, who stayed late" (all of them) differ.

**Tense:**

- Keep tense consistent. Shift only for a real change in time.
- Use the present tense for general truths, and for what a document or author says ("The 10-K
  *discloses*...", "Orwell *argues*...").
- Use the past perfect for the earlier of two past events. Don't use the future tense
  needlessly.

**Mood:**

- Use the subjunctive for conditions contrary to fact ("If we *were* to close today...").
- Use it after *insist*, *request*, and *propose*: "insisted that she *be* the lead."
- If the condition is real, use the indicative.

## 4. Punctuation: the rules that catch most errors

English uses about a dozen marks. Read `references/usage.md` for each one. The
high-frequency rules:

- **Commas, which are not pauses.** Use them:
  - before *and*, *but*, *or*, *nor*, *so*, *yet*, or *for* joining two independent clauses
    (it may be dropped when both are short and clear);
  - after a long introductory clause or phrase, and after any absolute phrase;
  - around nonrestrictive elements, appositives, direct address, and interrupting transitions
    ("The forecast, however, assumes...", but "We therefore propose...");
  - before the last item of a series (the **serial comma**, which APA requires and Chicago recommends);
  - between coordinate adjectives that could be reversed or joined by *and*.

  **No comma** between a subject and its verb or a verb and its object, right after a
  conjunction, ahead of a series or after its last item, or between the last adjective and
  the noun it describes.
- **Semicolons:**
  - join two balanced independent clauses;
  - go before a conjunctive adverb, which then takes a comma ("; however,");
  - separate series items that contain commas.

  A semicolon never links a dependent clause to a main clause, and never introduces a list
  (use a colon).
- **Colons** follow a complete independent clause to introduce what explains, lists, or
  renames it. No colon after a verb, a preposition, or *including*, *such as*, or *for
  example* before a simple list. After a colon, capitalize when two or more sentences or a
  formal statement or question follow; lowercase a subordinate element or a closely related
  thought. Formal salutations take a colon ("Dear Ms. Chen:").
- **Dashes** (em, —) give emphasis or mark an abrupt turn, and they enclose a parenthetical
  element that contains commas. Parentheses de-emphasize; commas are neutral. **En dashes**
  (–) mark ranges (slides 14–20, FY2023–FY2025).
- **Hyphens:**
  - Hyphenate a compound modifier *before* the noun ("long-term debt") but not after it ("debt that
    is long term").
  - No hyphen after an *-ly* adverb ("publicly traded company").
  - Use suspended hyphens in a series ("short-, medium-, and long-term notes").
  - Hyphenate *ex-* meaning "former", and a prefix before a proper noun ("pre-IPO").
  - Hyphenation can change meaning: *re-sign* (sign again) and *resign* (quit);
    *re-cover* and *recover*.
- **Apostrophes:**
  - Possession: *'s* on singular nouns; a bare apostrophe on plurals ending in *s*.
  - Compounds take the apostrophe at the end ("attorney general's").
  - Joint possession goes on the last noun only; individual possession goes on each noun.
  - No apostrophe for plural numbers or all-caps abbreviations (1990s, PDFs, KPIs).
- **Quotation marks:**
  - Commas and periods go **inside** the closing mark; colons and semicolons go **outside**.
    Question and exclamation marks go inside only if they belong to the quotation.
  - Never use quotation marks for emphasis. They signal a quotation, a special sense, or the
    title of a short work.

## 5. Mechanics

Read `references/usage.md` for the full rules (fractions, addresses, measurements,
italics, compound plurals, capitalization).
The core:

- **Numbers in words or numerals:**
  - Spell out zero through nine; use numerals for 10 and up.
  - Spell out any number that starts a sentence, or rewrite the sentence.
  - Spell out round, approximate numbers ("about two hundred").
  - In copy dense with numbers, use numerals throughout (except at the start of a sentence).
  - Never write a number in words and then repeat it in parentheses as a numeral.
- **Percentages:** use a numeral plus *percent* in text ("85 percent"); use % in tables.
- **Money:**
  - Numerals: "$60,000", "$28.95".
  - Words for indefinite amounts ("a few hundred dollars").
  - A numeral plus *million* or *billion* for round large amounts ("$6.8 million").
  - Exact figures in full ("$2,452,500").
- **Dates:**
  - Write "June 30, 2027," with a comma after the year in mid-sentence. "30 June 2027"
    takes no commas, and neither does "May 2026."
  - Never write a date all in numerals (11/6/26 means June 11 in much of the world).
  - Don't use ordinals in written dates ("March 4", not "March 4th").
- **Time:** "7:30 a.m." in the 12-hour system; "0730" in the 24-hour system, with no colon.
- **Abbreviations:**
  - When in doubt, spell it out.
  - Define at first use: "Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)". In a long document,
    remind the reader periodically.
  - Acronyms take capitals and no periods.
  - Uppercase initialisms take no periods (GDP, IRA), except U.S., E.U., and degrees (M.B.A.).
  - Plurals take *s* without an apostrophe (DCFs).
  - Don't repeat the last word of an abbreviation ("ATM", not "ATM machine"), and don't
    invent abbreviations.
  - A sentence ending in an abbreviation takes only one period.
- **Company names:** follow the official name. Write *Inc.*, *Corp.*, or *&* only if the
  company does.
- **Capitalization:**
  - Capitalize proper nouns, but not the class they belong to: Microsoft, but the company.
  - Capitalize a title before a name (Senator Warren). Lowercase a descriptive appositive after
    a name (Elizabeth Warren, senator from Massachusetts).
  - Capitalize a job title used with a name ("Ana Ruiz, Chief Financial Officer"). Lowercase it
    alone ("the chief financial officer").
  - Capitalize an organization's own departments (Human Resources Department), but not a type
    of organization.
  - Capitalize regions but not directions; months and days but not seasons.
  - Capitalize numbered units (Chapter 5, Room 72) but not minor ones (line 11).
  - Don't capitalize a sentence set inside parentheses within another sentence.

## 6. Numbers in financial writing (this skill's addition)

The general rules plus the conventions finance readers expect. The house style or the
assignment's style wins when one exists.

- **Consistency beats any single rule.** Pick one scale per document or table ($M or $B) and
  one number of decimals per metric, and keep them.
- **Label units every time a reader could lose them**: "$4.2 billion in revenue", "EPS of
  $3.15", "a 12.5x EV/EBITDA multiple."
- **Percent vs. percentage points.** A margin moving from 20% to 22% rose **2 percentage points**
  (200 basis points), which is a **10 percent** increase. Never write "rose 2%" for this.
- **Growth rates name the period and the basis**: "revenue grew 8% year over year", "a 5-year
  CAGR of 6%."
- **Fiscal vs. calendar years:** write FY2025 (or FY25) and define the fiscal year-end once.
  Use an en dash for ranges (FY2023–FY2025).
- **Negative numbers** go in parentheses in tables, ($1.2M), and in words in prose ("a loss of
  $1.2 million").
- **Ranges:** repeat the unit or symbol on both ends to avoid ambiguity ("$40–$45 per share").
- **Rounding:** check that the components sum to the stated total. If they don't because of
  rounding, say so in a note.
- **Every number traces to a source or a model cell.** Proofread numbers against the source,
  not against memory. This belongs to the second stage of §1.

## 7. Commonly confused words

Read `references/usage.md` for more pairs. The ones that most often matter in business and
finance:

| Pair | The difference |
| --- | --- |
| affect / effect | *affect* is usually the verb (to influence); *effect* is usually the noun (a result), or a verb meaning "to bring about" |
| fewer / less | *fewer* goes with count nouns (fewer errors); *less* with mass nouns (less risk) |
| amount / number | *amount* for mass (amount of cash); *number* for count (number of shares) |
| that / which | *that* is restrictive (no commas); *which* is nonrestrictive (with commas) |
| i.e. / e.g. | *i.e.* = "that is" (restates); *e.g.* = "for example" (gives an instance). Each is followed by a comma |
| imply / infer | the writer implies; the reader infers |
| ensure / insure / assure | *ensure* = make certain; *insure* = cover with insurance; *assure* = reassure a person |
| principal / principle | *principal* = main, or the capital sum; *principle* = a rule or belief |
| compose / comprise | the parts compose the whole; the whole comprises the parts (never "comprised of") |
| average / median / mean | *mean* is the arithmetic average; *median* is the middle value. Say which you mean |
| percent / percentage | *percent* goes with a number (12 percent); *percentage* goes without one (a large percentage) |
| due to / because of | *due to* follows a linking verb (the delay was due to...); *because of* modifies a verb (delayed because of...) |
| its / it's | *its* is possessive; *it's* = it is |
| discreet / discrete | *discreet* = tactful; *discrete* = separate |
| biannual / biennial | *biannual* = twice a year; *biennial* = every two years |

## 8. Easy to confuse

- **Comma splice vs. run-on:** a splice has a comma between the clauses; a run-on has nothing.
  The fixes are the same.
- **Dangling vs. misplaced modifier:** a dangling modifier has no word to attach to; a
  misplaced one attaches to the wrong word.
- **Dash vs. hyphen vs. en dash:** an em dash (—) marks emphasis or an interruption; a hyphen
  (-) joins words; an en dash (–) marks a range.
- **Colon vs. semicolon:** a colon introduces something; a semicolon balances two clauses of
  equal weight.
- **Proofreading vs. revising:** proofreading corrects the surface; revising changes content
  and structure. If proofreading finds a structural problem, stop and revise.

## 9. Delivering the pass

- For a user's draft, return the corrected text plus a short list of the **non-obvious**
  changes, with the rule behind each ("that → which: nonrestrictive clause"). Don't list every
  comma.
- Flag, rather than silently change, anything that changes the meaning (a restrictive vs.
  nonrestrictive comma, a misplaced *only*) and any number you couldn't verify against its
  source.
- Respect the user's voice. Mechanics is not a license to rewrite style; that is
  `line-editing`'s job.
