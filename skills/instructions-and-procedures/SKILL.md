# Instructions and Procedures

Instructions are judged by one test: can someone who has never done the task follow them,
alone, without asking? Policies, procedures, forms, and job descriptions are judged the same
way. Each is read by a person trying to *do* something, often while doing it.

Sources: standard technical-writing practice (see ATTRIBUTION.md at the repo root) and the
US EPA's *Guidance for Preparing Standard Operating Procedures* (EPA QA/G-6), a public-domain
government work. The finance additions in §7 and the "easy to confuse" list are this
skill's own, labeled as such.

Read `references/templates.md` when drafting an SOP or a job description from a blank page.

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and
`grammar-and-mechanics` (pass 7), before you deliver.

## When to Use

- Writing or reviewing instructions, a how-to, onboarding steps, or a checklist
- Explaining how a process works (for understanding rather than for doing)
- Drafting a policy, a procedure, or an SOP; turning a policy into steps
- Designing a form, an intake or registration form, or a feedback form
- Writing a job, role, or position description

## 1. Instructions

Clear instructions build goodwill: they let readers finish tasks efficiently and prevent
mistakes. You must understand the process or system thoroughly before you can explain it.
The best instructions combine words and visuals that reinforce each other.

**Know the readers.** Consider the knowledge of both primary and secondary readers. Expert
readers can take specialized terms; for anyone else, use plain language, or define the
unavoidable terms in a glossary.

**Write the steps:**

- **Imperative mood, active voice, present tense** ("Open the cover", not "The cover should be
  opened").
- **Clarity beats brevity.** Dropping articles, pronouns, and verbs shortens a sentence into
  telegraphic style that readers can't decode ("Attach approved form w/ receipts, fwd to AP").
  Write the full sentence: which form, approved by whom, sent where.
- **Short, simple steps in the right order,** marked by sequence words (*first, next, then,
  finally*) or, better for most tasks, **numbers** (word processors' numbered-list feature).
- **Plan ahead for the reader:** if step 2 affects step 9, say so at step 2.
- **Simultaneous actions** go in one step, or the introduction says they must be done together:
  "While holding Shift, select the last row", not two steps.
- **Warn before the hazard,** never after the reader has reached the step.
- **Many steps → stages,** each with its own heading and its own numbering from 1. Headings
  matter most when the reader is doing the task while reading.

**Illustrate.** Develop visuals together with the text, especially for complex tasks:
drawings, flowcharts, infographics, maps, photos. Use layout to make key visuals and text
stand out (boxes, bold, distinctive headings; test font style, size, and color).

**Test.** Have someone unfamiliar with the task follow the instructions while you watch. A
first-time user finds the missing steps and the unclear wording.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Imperative mood and active voice
- [ ] Short sentences, simple present tense
- [ ] No jargon the reader may not know, including undefined abbreviations
- [ ] **No elegant variation:** one word for one thing throughout
- [ ] No ambiguity
- [ ] Effective visuals and highlighting
- [ ] Tested by watching someone follow them
- [ ] A warning before every hazardous step

## 2. Process explanation

A process explanation describes how something works or gets done (how a wire transfer
clears) for **understanding**, not for the reader to perform right away.

- The introduction gives an overview of the process, or why the reader should understand it.
- Define terms readers may not know; add visuals to clarify.
- Use time transitions inside paragraphs for unity, and headings as the transitions from one
  step or stage to the next.
- Write it in the indicative ("The employee submits..."), not the imperative.

## 3. Policies and procedures

A **policy** states the organization's position on a subject. A **procedure** gives the steps
for carrying it out. They're usually written together, by top or middle managers, and reviewed
carefully, often by legal staff, so they need careful planning and exact wording (`line-editing`
on plain language).

**Policies:**

- Optionally open with the policy's **purpose or rationale**.
- Then the details in **numbered sections** (decimal numbering: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3), so each rule
  can be cited: who is covered, what is allowed, the limits and exceptions, and the defined
  terms.
- Keep them where they can be found and updated (a binder or the intranet).

**Procedures:**

- Give the step-by-step for carrying out the policy, often for **managers** as well as
  employees, since managers must enforce it.
- **The actor/action chart:** before drafting, draw two columns. Under **Actor**, write who
  acts at each step; under **Action**, write the full step, in order. The chart becomes the
  outline. For example, an expense-approval procedure:

| Actor | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Employee | Submits the expense report with receipts within 30 days |
| Manager | Approves or returns it within five business days |
| Accounts payable | Checks receipts against the policy limits; returns exceptions to the employee |
| Accounts payable | Schedules payment in the next weekly run |

- Then draft in numbered sub-sections (2.1, 2.1.1, 2.1.2), each naming the actor and the
  action.

## 4. Forms

A form gathers information in a standard design. A good one is quick for the respondent to
fill in and quick for the person processing it to read, record, and interpret, and a
first-time user can complete it without help. Decide what information you need, order the
requests logically, and **test it on people from the target audience** before distributing it.

**Ethics.** Information on forms can be sensitive, personal, or legally protected. Ask in a
way that is neither invasive nor illegal; respondents expect confidentiality unless the form
says otherwise. When unsure, check the organization's privacy policy.

**Paper or digital:**

- **Paper** helps in some manufacturing and service settings and at in-person events.
- **Digital** suits registrations, applications, and orders: it standardizes the interface,
  feeds a database that tabulates the data, can be encrypted, and can refuse submission until
  required fields are complete.

**Design:**

- **Top:** organization name, form title, any reference number.
- **Instructions:** at the start of the form or of each section, set off with headings, bold,
  or shaded boxes. For paper forms, put submission and copy-distribution instructions at the
  **bottom of each page**, and leave room for signatures and dates.
- **Digital:** end with a Submit button that records the data, opens a confirmation page, or
  sends a confirming email.

**Entry types:**

- **Writing line:** a rule with a caption ("Employee Name").
- **Writing block:** the same, but each entry in a ruled box, so respondents can't attach a
  caption to the wrong line.
- **Check boxes:** when you can anticipate every answer, print the question and a labeled box
  per answer. Easy to fill in and to tabulate.
- **Digital fields:** text boxes, radio buttons, drop-downs, lists, check boxes, aligned in
  table cells or groups. Every field gets a label. **Labels go left of text boxes, drop-downs,
  and lists; right of radio buttons and check boxes.** Mark required fields.

**Sequencing:**

1. Open with easy questions.
2. Follow the subject's own logic (a travel-reimbursement form runs chronologically through
   the trip).
3. Put any item that depends on another after it.
4. Group related requests.

**Questions:**

- **Open-ended:** the respondent's own words. Use them for answers you can't anticipate (a
  complaint form) or too many to list; they're harder to tabulate.
- **Closed-ended,** for standardized, easy-to-tabulate answers. Four types:
  1. **Multiple choice:** one (or more) from a list.
  2. **Ranked choice:** order items by preference.
  3. **Forced choice:** one of two (yes/no).
  4. **Likert:** a point on a numbered scale (1 = very unsatisfied ... 5 = very satisfied).
- Keep questions simple and specific, with room for an adequate answer.
- **Captions:** brief; group related items under one heading instead of a question per line
  ("Bank details: Bank name, Routing number, Account type", not three questions). Be specific: "Effective date" or "Date issued", never a bare "Date" when it isn't
  today's date.

## 5. Job descriptions

Formal job descriptions set out a position's duties and requirements. They support equitable
pay scales, show management whether every function is covered, and tell current and
prospective employees exactly what's expected. Together, a company's job descriptions picture
its structure. **A job description describes the job, never the person in it.**

**Four common sections:**

1. **Accountability:** the title (only) of the person the job reports to.
2. **Scope:** what the role is mainly and secondarily for, and who reports to it.
3. **Specific duties:** a detailed, concise list.
4. **Requirements:** required or preferred education, training, experience, and licenses.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] List every task you do over a week or a month first, or you'll leave some out.
- [ ] Describe the job, not yourself.
- [ ] Duties in **decreasing order of importance** (which also makes valid qualifications
      easier to set).
- [ ] **Start each duty with a verb,** specifically: "Reconcile the operating accounts
      weekly", not "Account reconciliation".
- [ ] Study well-regarded existing descriptions.

*Position description* is a common synonym, and can also mean a posted announcement of an
open role.

## 6. Easy to confuse

- **Instructions vs. process explanation:** instructions tell the reader what to do
  (imperative, numbered); a process explanation tells how something happens (indicative,
  paragraphs with transitions).
- **Policy vs. procedure:** the rule and its rationale vs. who does what, in order.
- **Procedure vs. instructions:** a procedure coordinates several actors around a policy; an
  instruction set guides one user through a task.
- **Form vs. questionnaire:** a form collects the facts needed to process something; a
  questionnaire collects opinions or data for research (`research-and-sources`).
- **Job description vs. job ad:** the internal record of a position vs. a recruiting document
  (`career-writing`).
- **Writing line vs. writing block:** a captioned rule vs. a captioned, fully ruled box.

## 7. Finance additions (this skill's own)

- **SOPs and internal controls:** in a finance function, a procedure doubles as a control
  record. For each step, name the **actor** (the actor/action chart), the **evidence** left
  behind (a signed approval, a ticket), and any **segregation of duties** (the person who
  requests a payment is never the one who approves it). Auditors read procedures this way.
- **Month-end close checklist:** a sequential procedure with an owner, a due day (day 1, day
  2...), the dependency, and a sign-off column per task. Same principle as the Gantt chart in
  `visuals`.
- **Desk procedures:** write recurring analyst tasks (refreshing a model, pulling filings,
  updating comps) as numbered instructions with exact file paths, data sources, and checks, and
  test them by having a colleague run them once.
- **Approval-matrix policies** (who can approve how much) belong in a table, with amounts and
  roles, not prose.
- **Forms with personal or financial data:** collect only what you need; follow the firm's
  data-privacy rules; never ask for full account numbers or government ID numbers in an
  unencrypted form or email.

## 8. Before it goes out

- [ ] Written for the least-expert reader who will use it; terms defined
- [ ] Imperative, active, present tense for steps; one action per step (or deliberately
      combined)
- [ ] Steps numbered, in order, grouped into headed stages if long
- [ ] Forward references and warnings placed before they're needed
- [ ] One term per thing throughout
- [ ] Visuals integrated where words struggle
- [ ] Tested by someone new to the task, and fixed where they stumbled
- [ ] For policies and procedures: numbered sections, actor named in each step, legal review
      if needed
- [ ] For forms: preliminary information, clear captions, logical order, tested, privacy
      respected
- [ ] For job descriptions: four sections, verb-first duties, decreasing importance
- [ ] Proofread (`grammar-and-mechanics`)
