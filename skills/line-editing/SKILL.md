# Line Editing

Use this skill for the line-editing pass of revision (step 6, pass 4, of `business-writing`). Editing is a large part of
good writing. Cutting words that weigh the prose down and make the writer sound uncertain
gives the writing confidence.

These are rules of thumb, not a religion. Apply them with judgment (see rule 4 under
"Key rules").

## When to Use

- The line-editing pass of any draft, after structure and premise are settled
- Tightening wordy or hedged prose
- Deciding whether a sentence is passive, or whether a series is parallel

Line editing can't fix a wrong purpose, a wrong premise, or the wrong medium. If the draft
has those problems, go back to `business-writing` first. Grammar, punctuation, numbers, and
word-pair questions (*affect/effect*) belong to the next pass, `grammar-and-mechanics`.

## Cut wordiness

### 1. Weasel words
Weasel words are qualifiers that make the writer sound unsure, as if they want wiggle room:
- like
- sort of, kind of
- maybe, perhaps
- might, can

Some contexts need them, for example an honest estimate or a genuinely uncertain forecast.
When the text states an idea or makes an argument, remove them.
- Weak: "Maybe it can make a difference."
- Strong: "It makes a difference."

### 2. Adverbs
Adverbs are like seasoning: a little goes a long way. Stephen King (*On Writing*): "The road
to hell is paved with adverbs." The common do-nothings:
- actually, basically, really, very, seriously
- currently, presently
- suddenly

Test: read the sentence without the adverb. If the meaning doesn't change significantly, cut it.
- Padded: "We do strongly recommend that you seriously think about taking out the adverbs,
  unless you actually need to significantly modify an idea."
- Clean: "We recommend taking out the adverbs unless you need to modify an idea."

### 3. "There is," "there are," and other empty openers
Openers made of "it," "here," or "there" plus a form of "to be" are empty filler. Start
instead with the subject, or better, a verb, to focus on the action and the idea.
- Weak: "There are many factors that affect the valuation."
- Strong: "Many factors affect the valuation."

## Replace signs of weakness

### 1. Excessive punctuation
Colons, semicolons, and dashes sometimes help, but shorter sentences usually work better.
If sentences are long and crammed with punctuation, split them.

### 2. Too many negatives
Watch for clusters of "shouldn't," "can't," "don't," and other forms of "not." Pick a verb
that doesn't need "not."
- Weak: "You shouldn't use negatives in your writing."
- Strong: "Use positive words in your writing."

### 3. Excessively fancy words
A precise long word can earn its place ("with equanimity" says more than "doing a good job
and staying calm"). Don't add big words to sound smart. Use them sparingly.

### 4. The word "thing"
Almost every "thing" can become a more specific word.
- Weak: "I'm trying to strengthen my writing with things that sound better to an audience."
- Strong: "I'm trying to strengthen my writing by gearing my style toward a target audience."

## Key rules

### 1. Make verbs stronger
Replace weak verb phrases with one strong verb ("make a decision" → "decide"; "conduct an
analysis of" → "analyze").

### 2. Think about icebergs
Hemingway: "The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being
above water." A writer who knows the subject well can omit what they know, and the reader
will still feel it. Cut what you *want* to say down to what you *need* to say. Show, don't
tell, and when you show, show it briefly.

### 3. Follow Orwell's six rules ("Politics and the English Language")
1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an
   everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

In short: keep the writing simple, brief, active, free of clichés, and to the point.

Note on rule 5: in finance and other specialist writing, a term the reader uses daily
(EBITDA, DCF, basis points) is not jargon to that reader. Step 1.2 of `business-writing`
(audience) decides this, not a blanket ban.

### 4. Use your own best judgment
Sometimes a grand word or even "thing" is the right choice. Keep these rules in mind while
polishing, but let the purpose and reader decide.

## Style and tone

Standard style rules, consistent with the US federal plain-language guidelines and the SEC's
*A Plain English Handbook*. Read `references/plain-language.md` for the plain-language
checklist (both public-domain US government works), with a wordy-phrase table, paragraph
unity and transitions, and a before-and-after finance example. The rules that change most
drafts:

- **Match the style to the reader.** Business style sits between a text message and a
  contract. Too formal alienates the reader; forced informality sounds insincere, especially
  to clients and strangers. No contractions in reports, proposals, or formal letters.
- **Conciseness is not brevity.** Cut redundancy ("final outcome", "each and every"), excess
  qualification, and circumlocution. Then stop before the prose turns choppy or telegraphic.
  Dropping articles and transitions ("Rcvd Q3 file. Numbers off. Pls fix.") makes
  readers decode, and it can make the text ambiguous.
- **Replace the wordy phrase:** *in order to* → *to*; *due to the fact that* → *because*;
  *by means of* → *by*; *at this point in time* → *now*.
- **Unbury the verb.** A nominalization plus a weak verb ("perform an analysis", "make a
  recommendation") becomes the verb ("analyze", "recommend").
- **Say the positive thing positively, and the negative thing negatively.** "Send it once legal
  approves" beats "Don't send it until legal hasn't objected." Never dress a negative fact as a
  positive one: "nearly reached our target" for "missed it by 8 percent" misleads. Don't state
  bad news more harshly than it is ("Your order ships the day payment clears").
- **"You" viewpoint, with care.** Frame around the reader's benefit ("So your refund
  arrives this week, please..."). Drop "your" where it sounds accusatory ("The forecast should
  include currency costs", not "Your forecast ignores currency costs").
- **I vs. we.** *I* is your opinion; *we* commits your organization. Never refer to yourself as
  "the writer" or "one". Use the impersonal view when tact or a group audience needs it
  ("Some team members have raised concerns about the timeline").
- **Emphasis, through structure first.**
  - Use the first and last positions of a sentence or paragraph.
  - Put items in climactic order.
  - Place a short sentence after long ones.
  - Use the active voice and a direct statement ("Most important, ...").
  - Use italics, bold, capitals, and exclamation marks sparingly.
- **Subordinate the lesser idea** so the main one stands in the main clause. Avoid chains of
  overlapping *which* clauses.
- **Vary sentence length and openings.** Use short sentences for emphasis and long ones for
  explanation.
- **Repeat the key term** rather than cycling through synonyms ("the deal... the transaction...
  the acquisition"), which makes readers wonder whether these are different things.
- **Plain language over affectation.** Prefer *use* to *utilize* and *orient* to *orientate*.
  Avoid *aforesaid*, *herewith*, legalese, buzzwords (*touch base*, *impact* as a verb), and
  clichés (*game plan*, *last but not least*). Jargon is fine only when every reader shares
  it.
- **Concrete over vague.** "The committee approved the Q3 budget", not "It was a good
  meeting". Words like *good*, *important*, *nice*, and *thing* need a specific
  replacement.
- **Euphemisms and biased language.** Don't use euphemisms to hide facts ("rightsizing" for
  "layoffs"). Use gender-neutral job titles (*chair*, *salesperson*) and parallel terms for men
  and women. Don't mention differences among people unless they are relevant.

## Active vs. passive voice

### How to tell them apart
- **Active:** the subject performs the action. "Steve loves Amy." "I heard it through the grapevine."
- **Passive:** the target of the action becomes the subject. "Amy is loved by Steve." "It was
  heard by me through the grapevine."
- A form of "to be" does **not** make a sentence passive. "I am holding a pen" is active; the
  passive version is "The pen is being held by me."
- The test: is the subject performing the action? If the subject is only receiving it, the
  sentence is passive.
- Caution: Strunk & White's *The Elements of Style* mislabels several of its own examples of
  passive voice, so don't rely on it for identification.

### Why to prefer active voice
Passive voice is not grammatically wrong, but it is often the weaker choice:
- It is usually wordier.
- It can be awkward or vague.
- It makes it easy to drop the actor. "Amy is loved" doesn't say by whom.
- It can obscure responsibility, deliberately or not. "Mistakes were made" (Reagan, on
  Iran-Contra); "shots were fired."
- Some readers find passive sentences harder to understand, which matters when writing for a
  general audience.

In business writing, hiding the actor usually costs credibility. Name who did what.

### When passive voice is the right choice
- **The actor is unknown.** "The store was robbed."
- **The target deserves the emphasis.** "The cookies were stolen" puts the focus on the cookies.
- **Tact.** A company may prefer "Your electricity will be shut off" over "We, the electric
  company, will be shutting off your power."
- **Objectivity in scientific writing.** Even there, active alternatives often work: "We
  sequenced the DNA," or "The data suggest that the mutation causes cancer" instead of "We
  believe the mutation causes cancer."

## Parallel structure

Parallel structure uses the same grammatical pattern for two or more ideas of equal
importance. It applies to words, phrases, and clauses, and the elements are usually joined
by coordinating conjunctions such as "and" or "or."
- Not parallel: "The role involves building models, client meetings, and to write memos."
- Parallel: "The role involves building models, meeting clients, and writing memos."

Apply it to every series in a sentence, every item in a list, and every heading at the same level.
