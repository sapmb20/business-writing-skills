# Plain language

Part of `line-editing` (revision pass 4). The checklist below condenses two US government
guides, which are works of the federal government and in the public domain in the United
States (17 U.S.C. § 105):

- U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Office of Investor Education and Assistance.
  (1998). *A Plain English handbook: How to create clear SEC disclosure documents*.
  https://www.sec.gov/pdf/handbook.pdf (its preface by Warren Buffett is not used here).
- Plain Language Action and Information Network. (2011). *Federal plain language
  guidelines*, now maintained by the U.S. General Services Administration at
  https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language.

Paraphrased and reorganized for business and finance writing; the examples are this repo's
own. Neither agency endorses this file.

---

## The checklist

**Before writing**
- [ ] Know who reads it and what they need to do with it. Write for that reader, not for
      the reviewer, the lawyer, or yourself.
- [ ] Know the main message, and put it first.

**Organization**
- [ ] Order the content the way the reader uses it: most important first, or in the order
      of the task.
- [ ] Use headings that say what the section says, often as questions or statements.
- [ ] Keep paragraphs short and about one topic, each opening with its topic sentence.
- [ ] Use lists and tables for steps, conditions, and comparisons.

**Sentences**
- [ ] Prefer short sentences: one main idea each, averaging around 20 words or fewer.
- [ ] Use the active voice, with the actor as the subject.
- [ ] Use strong verbs, not nouns made from verbs ("we analyzed", not "we performed an
      analysis of").
- [ ] Use "you" for the reader and "we" for the organization when it fits the document.
- [ ] Keep subject, verb, and object close together; move long qualifiers to the end or to
      their own sentence.
- [ ] State things positively; avoid double negatives and exceptions to exceptions.

**Words**
- [ ] Choose everyday words over formal ones (see the table).
- [ ] Use the same term for the same thing throughout; define a technical term once, the
      first time it appears.
- [ ] Cut legal and bureaucratic filler ("herein", "aforementioned", "pursuant to").
- [ ] Prefer concrete words to abstractions ("a 3% fee", not "certain charges").
- [ ] Avoid hidden verbs and superfluous words (see the table).

**Design and testing**
- [ ] Use white space, consistent type, and emphasis sparingly so the structure is visible.
- [ ] Test the draft: ask someone from the audience to find an answer or explain the main
      point back to you, and fix where they stumble.

| --- | --- |
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that, in light of the fact that | because |
| at this point in time, at the present time | now |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in the event that | if |
| with regard to, with respect to | about, on |

## Before and after (finance)

> **Before:** Pursuant to the terms of the agreement, it should be noted that in the event
> of a failure on the part of the borrower to make timely payment of any amount due, a late
> charge in an amount equal to 5% of the overdue amount may be assessed by the lender.
>
> **After:** If you pay late, we may charge you 5% of the overdue amount.

What changed: the reader became "you", the actor became "we", the condition moved to the
front, and the filler ("pursuant to", "it should be noted that", "on the part of") went.
Keep the precise terms (5%, overdue amount) that carry the legal meaning.

## Paragraphs: unity, coherence, transitions

- **Unity:** one paragraph, one idea, stated in the topic sentence; cut or move sentences
  that serve another idea.
- **Coherence:** each sentence picks up something from the one before (a repeated key term,
  a pronoun with a clear antecedent, a parallel structure).
- **Transitions** signal the relationship: addition (*also, in addition*), contrast
  (*however, but*), cause (*because, therefore*), example (*for example*), sequence
  (*first, next, finally*), conclusion (*so, as a result*). Use the one that states the true
  relationship, and don't stack them.
- **Length:** in business documents, paragraphs of three to six sentences are easy to scan;
  a one-sentence paragraph is fine for emphasis, rarely.
