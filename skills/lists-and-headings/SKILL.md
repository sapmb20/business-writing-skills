# Lists and Headings

Use this skill when outlining (step 4 of `business-writing`) and in the formatting pass of
revision (step 6, pass 5).

## When to Use

- Laying out a memo, report, proposal, or any document longer than a short email
- Choosing a list type, or punctuating and introducing a list
- Designing or reviewing a heading structure
- Page design beyond lists and headings: typeface and size, emphasis, justification, headers
  and footers (page numbers), columns, white space, and color (section "Page design" below)

## The pass, in order

1. **At outlining:** turn the outline's major divisions into headings (four levels at most),
   then mark the spots where a list would carry parallel items.
2. **For each list:** confirm it earns its place (General rules), choose the type, write the
   lead-in, then punctuate the items.
3. **At revision (pass 5):** check the headings against Principles, Do, and Don't, then the
   page design (last section).

## Lists

Used well, lists emphasize important ideas, simplify long sentences and paragraphs, and add
white space that makes reading easier. The wrong kind of list, or a badly formatted one,
creates confusion instead.

### General rules
- **Include** 2 to 8 items. One item isn't a list. More than 8 dilutes emphasis: if you
  emphasize everything, you emphasize nothing. (NASA caps emergency procedures at 8 steps.)
- **Avoid** splitting a list across two pages when possible.
- **Avoid overusing** lists. Every list needs explanatory text around it that says what it is
  a list of and why it's needed. A page of lists reads like an outline, not a coherent argument.
- **Adjust** spacing before, after, and within lists so they don't form a dense block.
- **Capitalize** the first letter of each item.
- **Use** parallel phrasing for every item (see `line-editing`).
- **Never use** a heading to introduce a list. Use a lead-in sentence.

If a list would only complicate the document, write the ideas as sentences within the paragraph.

### Choosing the type
- **Bullet list:** the order of items doesn't matter. Keep items short (a word or a phrase).
  If items run longer, use a labeled or nested list.
- **Numbered list:** the order matters, as in steps of instructions or ideas that the text
  will discuss in sequence. For more than 8 items, break the list into stages
  (Steps in Stage 1, Steps in Stage 2, etc.).
- **In-sentence list:** keeps paragraph style for 2 to 4 short items, marked (a), (b), (c).
  Use it to (a) keep the paragraph flowing, (b) avoid too many lists on one page, and (c)
  handle short items without creating a run-on.
- **Labeled list:** items need explanation. Start each with the label in bold and/or italics,
  followed by a colon, then the explanation in body text. Phrase the labels consistently and
  keep the explanations roughly equal in length and detail.
  - **Presentation 1:** Individual or pair presentation on a technical writing topic (worth 5%)
  - **Presentation 2:** Team presentation giving a progress report on Report 3 (worth 10%).
- **Nested list:** items fall into categories. Group a long flat list (for example, 12
  beverage containers) under category items ("Hot beverage containers," "Cold beverage
  containers") so the emphasis isn't lost.

### Punctuating list items
- Legal writing tends to end items with semicolons and add "and" before the last item.
- Technical and business writing favors simplicity: put a period only after the final item.
- If items contain complete sentences, end every item with a period.
- A simple bullet list may omit the final period.

### Lead-ins
Introduce every list with a lead-in sentence or clause that has a subject and a verb. The
phrase "the following" signals that a list is coming.
- If the lead-in is a complete sentence (it could end in a period), end it with a colon.
- If the lead-in is not a complete thought, use no punctuation, and make each item complete
  the lead-in grammatically.
- Rule of thumb: **if you can't put a period there, you can't put a colon there.**
  - Wrong: "The memo must include: a thesis, a valuation, and a recommendation."
  - Right: "The memo must include a thesis, a valuation, and a recommendation."
  - Right: "The memo must include the following elements:" followed by the list.

## Headings

Headings give an overview of the document, show the logical development and hierarchy of
ideas, let readers scan and read selectively, add white space, and let screen readers
navigate the text.

### Principles
- **Descriptive, not functional.** Headings should describe the section's content ("Margin
  Expansion Drives the Upside Case"), not its function ("Discussion"). Function headings suit
  fixed-structure documents such as lab reports; most business documents are not so predictable.
- **Hierarchy.** Use size, weight, typography, and color to show relative importance. First-level
  headings are larger and bolder than lower levels.
- **Consistency.** If one section has a heading, every section does. Headings at the same
  level share a design and use parallel phrasing.
- **Readability.** Leave slightly more space above a heading than below it. In short reports,
  aim for about 2 to 4 headings per page.

### Do
- Use a sans serif font for headings.
- Put a block of text under every heading.
- Build headings with the word processor's heading styles (Word's **Styles**), not manual
  bolding. Styles create an automatic table of contents, PDF bookmarks, the Navigation Pane,
  outline view, and a heading hierarchy that screen readers can follow.
- Use decimal numbering (1, 1.1, 1.1.1) with Arabic numerals if the document needs numbered
  headings. Don't use Roman numerals or letters.

### Don't
- **Stack headings.** Every heading needs at least a sentence under it before the next heading.
- **Overuse headings.** A heading defines a section, usually one or more paragraphs, not every paragraph.
- **Use a heading to introduce a table, figure, or list.** Text under the heading must introduce it.
- **Create lone headings.** If there's a 1.1, there must be a 1.2, just as a list needs two items.
- **Leave widows and orphans.** Don't strand a heading at the bottom of a page; insert a page break.
- **Refer to the heading as "this."** Start the section as if the heading weren't there, and
  don't open a section with a pronoun that points back to an earlier idea.

## Page design

Four standard design principles (proximity, contrast, repetition, alignment) cover most
business documents.

- **Proximity:** keep a heading closer to the text it introduces than to the text above it,
  and keep related items together.
- **Contrast:** make what matters look different (a bold heading, a shaded key figure), and
  use emphasis rarely enough that it still stands out.
- **Repetition:** the same heading styles, spacing, and list formats throughout. A change in
  form signals a change in meaning.
- **Alignment:** align text left and leave the right edge ragged for easy reading; line up
  headings, lists, and figures on a few shared edges.
- **Type:** one or two typefaces, body text around 10-12 points in print, and no long runs of
  capitals or italics.
- **White space:** generous margins and space between sections make a page look easy to
  read, so readers start reading.
- **Headers and footers:** page numbers on anything longer than two pages; a short title or
  date in the header of a long document.
