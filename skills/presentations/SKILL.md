# Presentations

A presentation is a document the audience hears once, in your order, at your pace, and
can't reread. Everything in this skill follows from that: purpose and audience come first,
as they do for writing, but the structure, the visuals, and above all the delivery have to
be built for listeners, which is why delivery gets as much weight here as content.

Sources: standard presentation practice as taught in business-communication courses and
open textbooks (see ATTRIBUTION.md at the repo root).

Slide design: David JP Phillips, "How to Avoid Death by PowerPoint" (TEDxStockholmSalon,
2014, https://youtu.be/Iwpi1Lm6dFo). He gives five slide-design principles drawn from
cognitive research on working memory and visual attention. They are in section 5 and are
marked **(Phillips)** wherever they appear.

The listener's-eye framing above, the recorded and AI-practice notes in section 8, and the
"easy to confuse" list are this skill's own.

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and
`grammar-and-mechanics` (pass 7), before you deliver.

## When to Use

- Planning a talk, pitch, briefing, or recorded presentation, from purpose statement to
  talking points
- Building, or reviewing, a slide deck
- Rehearsing, or getting feedback on a delivery, including a practice run with an AI tool
- A recorded presentation for a class or an application, or a practice run with an AI coach
- Planning or running a meeting, writing an agenda or minutes, or active listening

## 1. Purpose: know, believe, or do

Ask: **what should the audience know, believe, or do when I finish?** Write the answer as
a one-sentence purpose statement that answers both **what?** and **why?**

> This talk will show the finance committee that our month-end close takes three days
> longer than our peers' [what], so that they approve the reconciliation software in the
> Q2 budget [why].

- A purpose that's too broad is useless. "To explain our hiring needs" won't guide any
  decision. "To get the CFO to approve one hire by the March budget review" will.
- Purposes are often layered. A primary purpose (persuade management a problem exists)
  comes with a secondary goal (motivate them to act). Secondary goals are often abstract:
  to motivate, reassure, or inspire.
- In a group presentation, write the purpose statement **together** and keep returning to
  it; a team drifts faster than a solo speaker.

## 2. Audience: analyze the listeners

Ask these before you choose any content:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| How much do they already know about the topic? | Sets the level of detail and which terms you must define |
| Education, age, other demographics? | Shapes examples, vocabulary, and tone |
| **Attitude toward the topic**, and so their concerns, fears, or objections? | Tells you which objections the body has to answer |
| Are there subgroups with different concerns? | Executives want the decision; specialists want the method |
| What will they ask? | Build the answers into the talk (section 4) |

- With a mixed audience, serve the **primary** listeners first, usually the people who
  make the decision. Then serve the others, without short-changing the decision-makers.
- Workplace audiences usually know less about your specialty than you do. Define terms and
  principles you take for granted.
- If the audience is international, see section 10 (`references/cross-cultural.md`).

## 3. Gather information: enough, and no more

Collect the facts and arguments behind your position or proposal, and give
the audience **only what serves the goal**. Too much detail overwhelms them; too little
leaves them uninformed and your recommendation unsupported.

- Mix primary research (interviews, surveys, observation, your own data) with secondary
  research (articles, reports, databases).
- Judge every source on four tests: **authority, accuracy, bias, and currency**.
- Brainstorm to generate material: list everything, then ask who, what, when, where, why,
  and how of each idea. Cut what's redundant or irrelevant, and group the rest into a
  tentative outline. A cluster or mind map works well, including on a flip chart with a
  group.

## 4. Structure for listeners

Listeners are **freshest at the start** and **refocus near the end**. Use that pattern:

1. **Introduction:** an attention-getting opening, then an overview of what's coming
2. **Body:** develop the ideas
3. **Closing:** summarize what you covered, then, if it fits, a call to action

Outline first. A topic outline is enough for a short talk; a sentence outline makes each
line a candidate talking point. Plan the visuals inside the outline: mark "illustration
of..." where each one goes. Choose a **method of development** that suits the subject:

| Method | Use it when |
| --- | --- |
| **Order of importance, increasing** | Persuading: build to the strongest point and end on it |
| Problem → solution (cause and effect) | Something is broken and you are proposing the fix |
| Comparison | Choosing among options, or explaining something new through something familiar |
| Chronological or sequential | A timeline, an incident, or a process |
| Division and classification | Breaking a big topic into parts |
| General → specific, or specific → general | Leading up to, or down from, a conclusion |

Most talks blend methods: one primary method for the major divisions, with others under it.

### The introduction

Open with something that **focuses attention**. Five common kinds:

| Opening | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Definition of a problem | A pain the audience recognizes, then "here's a solution" |
| Attention-getting statement | A striking fact or statistic |
| Rhetorical question | "What would you do with three extra days every month?" |
| Personal experience | A short story from your own work or life |
| Appropriate quotation | A pertinent, credible quote, never a loosely related one |

Other openings that work aloud: the objective, the scope, background, the result up front,
a telling detail, a definition (only if it adds insight; never a dictionary quote), an
anecdote (best in longer talks), a forecast, and a persuasive hook.

Then give the **overview**. This can be the background the audience needs, or a preview of
how the talk is organized, such as "four parts: the problem, the options, the criteria, and
my recommendation." Write the introduction **last**, once you know what the body says.

### The body

- To persuade, present the evidence that will lead the audience to accept your conclusion
  and **act on it**.
- For a problem, first **prove it exists**, then offer a solution or range of solutions:
  prove the point, organize the facts and data strategically, present them in
  easy-to-understand visuals, offer solutions.
- **Anticipate questions** ("What does it cost per seat?") and **objections** ("Who maintains
  it after launch?"), and answer them inside the talk instead of waiting for Q&A.
- Persuasion rests on logic, facts, statistics, and examples. **Never make false claims.**
  Acknowledging the opposing view or the real downside builds credibility and disarms the
  objection.

### Transitions

Plan them in three places: **introduction → body**, **between major points**, and
**body → closing**. A transition is a sentence or two that closes one topic and opens the
next. It keeps the talk from feeling choppy and tells listeners you're in control of the
route. **Pause** after a transition so listeners can shift gears with you, because **they
do not know your plan.**

Ways to build one: transitional words (therefore, however, next, finally), repeated key
terms, enumeration ("the second criterion..."), summarizing the last point before starting
the next, or asking a question that the next section answers.

### The closing

The closing is **what the audience most likely remembers**, so make it strong.

- If the purpose is action, **ask them to do it**: specific, with a date ("approve the
  $18,000 by the March 15 budget meeting").
- If the purpose is to open minds, end by saying why your position is viable.
- A good closing **comes full circle**: it delivers on the purpose the introduction set.
- Other endings: recommendation, summary, judgment,
  implication, prediction, ideas for consideration, call for action, and a
  thought-provoking question.
- **Never introduce a new topic in the closing.**
- **Recall check (Phillips):** put the key points back to the audience as quick questions
  they can answer aloud ("How many messages per slide?"). Hearing themselves give the answer
  fixes the points in memory, and the check comes full circle on the overview.
- Put the asks on the last content slide as verb-first items with owners and dates. That
  slide becomes the circle-back email afterward (load `circling-back`).

## 5. Visuals and slides

Well-planned visuals add interest, focus, and emphasis, and charts and graphs greatly
improve understanding and retention of complex or technical information.

**Medium:** flip charts (small groups, brainstorming with the audience), whiteboards (quick
sketches and notes), or presentation software (PowerPoint, Prezi, and others) that combines
text, images, audio, links, and video. **Don't overuse effects.** Keep current with the
tool's features, and learn a new tool by experimenting, using its built-in help and
tutorials, and asking colleagues who know it.

**Choosing and building the chart or table itself** (which type, axes, scales, units, source
lines, misleading scales): load `visuals`. The slide rules below then apply on top.

### Slide design for the brain (Phillips)

An audience has one small working memory, and a busy slide uses it up. Five principles,
each one built into the checklist below:

1. **One message per slide**
2. **No sentences on screen while you speak**: the redundancy effect drives recall toward zero
3. **The most important element is the biggest one**, not the template title
4. **Contrast steers focus**: build-and-dim, highlight the key cell, and use a dark
   background for live talks
5. **Six objects at most**, with no page counters. More slides with fewer objects each
   beats fewer, fuller slides

Read `references/slide-design.md` for the reasons behind each principle, the
slide-count-vs-object-count argument, and general layout principles (proximity, contrast,
repetition, type). Read it whenever you build or review a deck.

### Slide checklist

- [ ] **One message** per slide, which the slide's biggest element carries (Phillips)
- [ ] **Six objects or fewer**, counting the title, logo, and footer. In a list, **numbers**
      if order matters, bullets if not
- [ ] **Phrases, not sentences.** The script goes in the notes. **Never read the slide
      aloud**: the audience can read it, and doing both at once wipes out recall
- [ ] Type readable from the back row; consistent font, size, and spacing on every slide
- [ ] Strong contrast between text and background. For a live talk, a dark background
      with light text (Phillips). Handouts and decks meant to be read keep a light background
- [ ] Builds that dim what was already covered are fine, because they steer focus. Avoid
      decorative sound, transitions, and animation
- [ ] Lists in **parallel structure** with balanced content (load `line-editing`)
- [ ] Graphs, charts, and infographics for data trends, with the key bar or cell
      highlighted
- [ ] No page numbers or "x of N" counters on screen
- [ ] Pace: plan the talk's length first, then split any dense slide into several and move
      through them faster
- [ ] **Match your words to the slide on screen**, never to the one before or after

### Integrity and backups

- **Credit every borrowed visual**, either on the slide or in a reference list you hand out
  (load `research-and-sources` for the citation format). Never compress a scale or drop
  data points to flatter a chart; a misleading visual is unethical.
- For detailed or complex material, give a **handout** they can take notes on.
- **Plan for technical failure.** If something breaks, stay calm and give yourself time to
  fix it, and if you can't, carry on without the technology. Always bring a printout of
  your slides, copies for the audience, and a backup of the file.

## 6. Rehearse

- Learn the sequence of your outline: major topics, notes, and visuals.
- **Practice on your feet and out loud**, in front of others if you can, and **on-site** if
  possible (lighting, equipment, seating, outlets, switches).
- Practicing aloud **times the talk**, exposes awkward transitions, and finds **verbal tics**
  ("um", "you know", "like") so you can cut them.
- **Practice with the visuals**, and run the equipment until it feels routine.
- Prepare notes that **trigger your memory**. They are cues, not a script.

## 7. Deliver

Delivery is aural **and** visual: nonverbal communication carries as much as the words. Be
animated, and **show enthusiasm** if you want listeners to share your view.

| Technique | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **Eye contact** | The best way to build rapport. In a big room, address the responsive faces in each section. Watch for cues, such as whether they look engaged, and adjust your pace |
| **Movement** | Take a step or two after a minute or so, ideally at transitions. **Don't pace.** At the screen, follow the **three t's: touch, turn, talk**: point, turn back to the audience, then speak |
| **Gestures** | Keep one hand free and use it; nervousness suppresses natural gesturing |
| **Voice** | Use inflection to separate key points from minor ones; a conversational delivery makes each listener feel addressed |
| **Projection** | Most speakers are quieter than they think. Rehearse with a listener in the back row |
| **Pace** | Too fast and words run together; too slow and listeners drift |

### Anxiety

Everyone gets nervous. Use the energy as a stimulant instead of letting it stop you. The
best cure is to **know the topic thoroughly**: when you know what you'll say and how you'll
say it, confidence follows.

### The audience is listening, not reading

Listening is active work: listeners have to focus, interpret, and judge. Help them with
signposts (the overview, transitions, summary), with repetition of key terms, and with
pauses. In Q&A, **respond rather than react**: hear the whole question, paraphrase it, then
answer.

## 8. Recorded, virtual, and AI-practice talks

- **Recorded:** rehearse with the recorder running, and watch one take back
  before the final one. Keep eye contact with the **camera lens**, not your own face on the
  screen. Put the slide on screen while you talk about it.
- **AI practice tool:** use the tool's feedback for the measurable things it is
  good at: pace, filler words, and timing against the limit. Check its comments on content
  against your purpose statement and the slide checklist. Save the feedback you act on, as
  evidence for the reflection.
- **Virtual delivery:** for Zoom, Teams, webinars, and the recorded takes above, load
  `virtual-presentations`: lighting, background, framing, the camera as eye contact, sound,
  backups, and engagement at least every 10 minutes.

## 9. Meetings, minutes, and listening

A meeting is planned like a talk: a purpose statement (what and why), only the people it
needs, an agenda sent a day or more ahead, and a minute-taker who isn't the leader and
records each assignment with an owner and a due date. Close by paraphrasing every decision and
assignment back to the room. Handle disruption by steering back to the agenda: thank a
rambler and restate the item, invite quiet people by name, and park off-topic issues for
later. Listen actively: give full attention, let the speaker finish, paraphrase what you
heard, and ask before you answer. Read `references/templates.md` for an agenda and minutes
template.

## 10. Cross-cultural audiences

When the audience is international or mixed-culture, read `references/cross-cultural.md`.
It covers directness, avoiding idioms and humor, dates, time zones and units, and what
colors, gestures, and symbols mean in different cultures. Test the talk with someone from
the audience's culture when you can.

## 11. Easy to confuse

| Pair | The difference |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose vs. topic | The topic is what you talk about; the purpose is what the audience should know, believe, or **do** afterward |
| Opening vs. overview | The opening grabs attention; the overview previews the route. The introduction needs both, in that order |
| Summary vs. call to action | A summary restates what you covered; a call to action asks for a specific act. A persuasive close usually does both |
| Transition vs. pause | A transition is the sentence; the pause after it is what lets listeners shift with you |
| Hearing vs. listening | Hearing is passive; listening is active. Design the talk for listeners who must work to follow |
| Responding vs. reacting | Responding means confirming the question before answering; reacting means blurting the first thought |
| Slide count vs. object count | Phillips: the number of objects per slide makes a deck hard to follow, not the number of slides. Fewer, fuller slides is the wrong fix |
| Highlight vs. animation | A build that dims what you've covered steers the eye, so use it. Decorative transitions and sounds only distract, so cut them |
| Notes vs. script vs. slide | Notes are cues that trigger memory; a script read aloud breaks eye contact. Full sentences go in the notes field, never on the slide, where they trigger the redundancy effect |
| Handout vs. backup printout | A handout is for the audience's notes; the printout is your fallback when the technology fails |

## 12. Pre-presentation checklist

- [ ] Purpose statement written, with both what and why
- [ ] Audience analyzed: knowledge, attitude, subgroups, likely questions and objections
- [ ] Evidence selected for the goal: not too much, not too little; sources pass authority,
      accuracy, bias, currency
- [ ] Opening chosen from the five; overview previews the points
- [ ] Body proves the problem, offers solutions, and answers objections in advance
- [ ] Transitions planned in all three places, with a pause after each
- [ ] Closing comes full circle, makes a specific ask, and adds nothing new; optional
      recall check on the key points
- [ ] Slides pass section 5's checklist: one message, six objects or fewer, the key element
      biggest, no sentences, dark background for a live talk, no page numbers; visuals
      credited; a handout if the content is dense
- [ ] Backups ready: printout, audience copies, and a second copy of the file
- [ ] Rehearsed aloud, on your feet, with the slides, and timed; filler words cut
- [ ] Notes are cues, not a script

## 13. How to deliver help

- **Talking points or an outline:** give the purpose statement first, then the structure
  as section headings (opening, overview, body points, closing ask), with the planned
  transition sentences written out.
- **Slides:** give one line per slide: the one message, then the content and the visual,
  with speaker notes holding any full sentences. Run the section 5 checklist on the result.
- **Reviewing an existing deck:** go slide by slide. Count the objects, name the message
  (or the two messages that need splitting), say what is biggest and whether it should be,
  and flag sentences, white backgrounds, and page counters. Then give the rewritten
  slide or slides.
- **Feedback on a recorded talk or deck:** organize it by the section 12 checklist, with the
  two or three changes that matter most first.
- **Online or recorded:** load `virtual-presentations` for the setup, camera, and engagement plan.
- **After the talk:** load `circling-back` to turn the closing's action items (usually the last
  slide) into the follow-up email.
- **A discussion post about a reading on presenting:** hand off to
  `business-writing` for the post itself, with this skill as the content source.
