# Slide design in depth

Part of `presentations`, section 5. The checklist in SKILL.md is the always-on rule set; this file holds the reasons and the detail behind it.

## Slide design for the brain (Phillips)

Phillips's point: an audience has one small working memory, and a busy slide uses it up.
When the slide is busy, most of what the speaker says is lost within seconds. Each principle
below removes a demand on that memory, so the listener's attention stays on you.

| # | Principle | Why it works | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **One message per slide** | Attention follows one thread at a time. With two messages on a slide, each listener picks one and misses the other | Split any slide that makes two points into two slides |
| 2 | **No sentences while you speak** (the redundancy effect, from Sweller and Mayer's research) | Reading text and hearing speech at once compete for the same channel, and recall falls to near zero | Put the script in the speaker-notes field. On the slide, keep short phrases plus an image |
| 3 | **Biggest = most important** | The eye goes first to what is big, high-contrast, signal-colored (red, orange, yellow), or moving. Default templates make the title the biggest object, so viewers spend most of their time on the least important text | Shrink the template title. Make the number, word, or chart that carries the message the biggest thing on the slide |
| 4 | **Contrast steers focus** | The eye follows whatever is brightest, the way a cat chases a laser dot | Reveal list items or table rows one at a time and dim the ones already discussed. Highlight one cell of a big table instead of showing it flat. For a live talk, use a **dark background** with light text: a white slide is the brightest and largest object in the room and pulls the eye away from you, and **you are the presentation** |
| 5 | **Six objects at most** | People take in up to about six items at a glance. Past that they have to count, which takes several times as long and as much effort, and a tired brain stops trying | Count everything on the slide: title, bullets, images, logos, footers. Delete page-number footers ("12 of 95"), which only tell people how much is left |

**More slides, fewer objects.** The number of slides was never the problem; the number of
objects per slide is. A cap like "four slides maximum" just crams the other slides' content
onto those four. Splitting busy slides makes the deck longer and the talk easier to follow.
In Phillips's example, a 95-slide deck became 135 slides. Pacing still matters (see the checklist in SKILL.md): more slides means each one is simpler, not that the talk is longer.

## General layout principles

These are standard graphic-design principles (see Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*),
applied to slides.

- **Proximity and alignment:** items that belong together sit together and share an edge.
- **Contrast:** make the one thing you want noticed look different from everything else, such
  as a single highlighted bar in a chart. Phillips's principles 3 and 4 apply this.
- **Repetition:** keep layout, heading style, and colors constant across the deck. A change in
  form reads as a change in meaning.
- Prefer a sans serif face on screens, use at most two typefaces, and avoid long runs of
  capitals or italics.
- Give every list a lead-in and keep its items parallel (load `lists-and-headings`).
