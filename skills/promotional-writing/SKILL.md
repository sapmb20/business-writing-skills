# Promotional Writing

A product nobody hears about doesn't sell. Promotional writing makes the case for a company,
product, or service to readers who owe it nothing, so it lives or dies on two things: what the
reader gets, and whether the reader believes you.

Sources: standard marketing-communication practice, including the AIDA model (attention,
interest, desire, action) and the inverted-pyramid news style (see ATTRIBUTION.md at the
repo root). The finance additions in §8 and the "easy to confuse" list are this skill's
own, labeled as such. Read `references/templates.md` for fill-in press-release and
sales-letter templates.

**Entry and exit.** Entered here directly? Load `business-writing` and run its steps
1.1-1.6 (purpose, audience, premise check, context gate) before drafting. When the draft is
done, return to its step 6: revision, including `line-editing` (pass 4) and

## When to Use

- Writing or reviewing a press release, sales letter or sales email, brochure, flyer,
  newsletter, or newsletter article
- Announcing a product, hire, milestone, or event to customers, the press, or members
- Drafting or revising a mission statement
- Adding a promotional angle to a routine document (an adjustment letter, a cover message)
- Reading a company's promotional material critically

## 1. Everyone writes promotion sometimes

You don't need to be in marketing to be asked for it, especially in a small firm or when
self-employed; even in a large one you may contribute to the website, a brochure, a
newsletter, a release, a sales letter, a blog, or a social feed. Promotion is also a
**secondary purpose** of many ordinary documents. An adjustment letter exists to fix a
problem, but it is also a chance to show the company at its best. These pieces are often
written by a team (see `business-writing` step 1.2 on team documents).

## 2. The promotional checklist (six points)

1. **Audience.** Analyze their needs, interests, concerns, makeup, and activities.
2. **Research.** Learn the product from people with firsthand knowledge of it (interviews:
   `research-and-sources`).
3. **Persuasion.** Move the reader through the four AIDA stages: **attention, interest,
   desire, action.** Desire is where you lower resistance with proof and benefits.
4. **Keywords.** Choose the words, phrases, and search tags that reach the most interested
   readers.
5. **Visual appeal.** Strong organization, layout, and visuals tied closely to the text.
6. **Clarity, coherence, conciseness,** so the reader understands and the purpose is met.

**Ethics.** Readers are persuaded only by a source they find credible. Don't overstate a
claim, and check the argument for logic errors (hasty generalization, false cause, and the
rest in `business-writing`'s persuasion reference).

## 3. Sales letters (and sales emails)

A sales letter promotes a product, service, or business, in print or digitally. It needs two
kinds of knowledge: the product, thoroughly, and the prospective customer's needs.

**The four moves.** An effective sales letter:

1. catches the reader's **attention**;
2. holds their **interest**;
3. **convinces** them the product meets a need or desire;
4. **asks confidently** for the action you want.

**Who gets it.** Start with your own customer or client list; then lists of people interested
in similar products. Marketing firms compile lists from associations and trade shows; they
cost money, so choose them carefully.

**Email or print.** Email is usually cheaper for large groups and can carry video and
purchase links, but it must stay short and targeted, and it falls under email-marketing law
(CAN-SPAM; see §8).

**Know the reader.** Seven traits shape the approach: gender, age, occupation, location,
education, financial situation, and interests.

**Psychological selling.** Sell the **benefit**, which may be intangible, not the physical
feature.

- Pick the **single strongest** selling point and build the letter around it.
- Show how the product makes the reader's work easier, raises their standing, or makes life
  more pleasant.
- Then present the features, each translated into what it does for the reader ("you"
  viewpoint, `line-editing`).
- Photos and links help the reader picture using it.

**Ethics.**

- Every claim must be valid. "Safe" promises absolute safety; qualify it ("safe when the
  normal precautions are followed").
- Point out real differences from competitors, but don't exaggerate them or disparage the
  competitor.

**Checklist.**

- [ ] The opening earns attention, for example with a feature aimed squarely at the reader's
      need.
- [ ] Proof that the product is what you say: case histories, a free trial, a money-back
      guarantee, testimonials, or endorsements (follow the FTC's rules; §8).
- [ ] Ways the reader could put it to use right away, with a brochure or a link to photos or
      video.
- [ ] **Price, put in context.** Common tactics:
  1. place the price **next to the benefit it buys**;
  2. quote the **smaller unit** ($12 a seat per month, not $14,400 a year for 100 seats);
  3. spread it over the product's **useful life** (about $1 a day);
  4. offer **payment plans**;
  5. **compare** it with a cost the reader already pays (one hour of overtime a week).
- [ ] **Easy response:** ordering instructions (online or phone), free delivery, discount
      codes.
- [ ] Social-media links, with an invitation to join the community around the brand.

## 4. Brochures

Short publications, usually folded, that promote a business and its products or services to
a target audience. They inform, persuade, or both, and they divide information into panels
better than a flyer can. Distribute them on paper or online, typically as PDFs.

**Two types:**

- **Sales brochures** sell specific products or services (a manufacturer's equipment and
  certifications; a consultant's services and seminars).
- **Informational brochures** educate without selling directly (a clinic explaining warning
  signs of a condition).

**Plan before writing.**

- Fix the purpose and the audience: new service for existing customers? A product for
  prospects? Benefits options for new employees?
- Match the fold to the content: a **trifold** gives six panels (good for several products);
  a **bifold** gives four larger panels (good for detailed schematics).
- Keep the style consistent with the organization's established brand, and put each piece
  of content where it makes sense.

**Panel-by-panel plan:**

| Panel | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **Front cover** | Win attention and show the purpose at a glance: headline, logo, one well-chosen image, very little text |
| **Back cover** | Full contact information, any legal or copyright disclaimers, and a map if readers need to find you |
| **First inside panel** | Identify the organization again and draw the reader in with headlines and brief text; usually the organization's "story" (history, accomplishments) or a guide to the panels that follow |
| **Later panels** | The product or service **from the reader's side**: benefits and solutions, backed by accurate facts, testimonials, and visuals (photos, cutaways); subheadings and bullets |
| **Final panel** | The exact action you want: call for a quote, order with the enclosed code, use the map to visit |

**Design checklist (eight items):**

- [ ] The content is consistent and logical.
- [ ] You looked at brochures for similar products or services first.
- [ ] A thumbnail sketch of each panel settled the panel count, text placement, and visuals.
- [ ] Margins, spacing, and text per panel leave enough white space; the most important
      information is easy to spot. Cut text if needed.
- [ ] Fonts vary with purpose (a different face for headlines or short testimonials) but
      unusual fonts and styles aren't overused.
- [ ] Colors match the brand. Full color can pay for high-end offerings; plain black and
      white suits internal pieces.
- [ ] Every image is accurate, high quality, and **properly licensed**; no clip art unless it
      suits the purpose.
- [ ] A plan exists to repurpose the brochure for digital access and social media.

## 5. Press releases

A press (news) release does two jobs: it **informs the public** about the organization and
its products or services, and it **strengthens the brand**. Large organizations have public
relations staff; in a small one, it may fall to you.

**Content rules:**

- Be clear and concise, and cover the **five W's**: who, what, where, when, why.
- **Start the first paragraph with a dateline:** the city, then the release date.
- Put **all the critical information in the first paragraph**, then order the rest by
  **decreasing importance**, so an editor can cut from the bottom.
- **Close with a short overview of the organization**: what it does, its products or
  services, and its locations (the "boilerplate").
- Get every fact right, and define any unfamiliar term.

**Distribution.** Online news outlets, local papers, TV and radio stations; the company's own
website and social accounts; trade and professional associations. Address it to a **specific
person**, such as the business or technology editor, or a blogger.

**Format checklist:**

- [ ] Company letterhead, margins of at least one inch
- [ ] Bold headline; double-spaced paragraphs, for easy reading and editing
- [ ] A professional photo if it helps (a head shot of a new hire, a product shot)
- [ ] "-more-" centered at the foot of any page that continues
- [ ] One blank line after the text, then "# # #", "-30-", or "-End-" centered to mark the end

## 6. Newsletters and newsletter articles

### Newsletters

Newsletters inform, and they build and keep interest and membership in an organization; some
also sell.

- **Organizational newsletters** go to employees, clients, or members: new products and
  policies, team and individual achievements. They enhance the group's image and build pride.
- **Subscription newsletters** build a paying readership around a product line or a subject
  (an experienced investor's market letter, a collector's guide). Subscribers are buying
  information and expect value for it.

**Developing one:**

- Decide the purpose and target audience; make the look and content reflect the readers'
  shared interests and values.
- Expect a team: design, content, and project management are often different people.
- Build the contact list and choose delivery: postal or interoffice mail, email, web or blog.
  A **list-hosting service** handles commercial email delivery.
- **Professionalism:** prune the subscriber list regularly, put an **opt-out** in every
  issue, and in email include opt-out instructions to comply with CAN-SPAM. Pestering former
  customers damages your reputation and the company's.
- **Find angles readers can't get elsewhere:** trade press and industry blogs, plus interviews
  and profiles of your own customers, members, and staff.
- **Check every fact.** Newsletter readers tend to be specialists.
- Issues often reach branches and clients abroad (see `business-writing` step 1.2 on
  international readers, and `visuals`).

**Format:**

- Simple and consistent, but visually appealing.
- Active voice, conversational tone, simple sentences, short paragraphs.
- Headings and bullets to break up text.
- Newspaper-style columns, with **one or two visuals per page** supporting the text.
- **First page:** a masthead (name, organization, date, and volume and issue), plus a short
  "in this issue" list.
- A web newsletter follows good web design (`writing-for-the-web`).

### Newsletter articles

Writing one raises the profile of your own work or department.

**Before writing:**

- Answer journalism's questions (**who, what, where, when, why**) and add **how**, which
  colleagues often care about most.
- Find out whether management has an official position on the subject, and follow it.
- Read several recent issues for style, tone, and approach.
- Ask **why this matters to colleagues and to the organization.** That answer decides the
  tone and, above all, the ending.
- Research mostly by interviewing the key people. Gather every point of view, and **credit as
  many people as possible**: quote those involved and name those who led the work.

**The pull quote.** An inset quotation in larger type draws readers into the article and
previews its content.

**Article checklist (six items):**

- [ ] An intriguing title; a rhetorical question often works.
- [ ] Eye-catching photos or visuals that pull the reader into the lead.
- [ ] A **lead (lede)**, the first paragraph, that bridges from the title into the body and
      makes the reader continue.
- [ ] A well-developed body that holds interest to the end.
- [ ] A conclusion that stresses why the subject matters to these readers and what they
      should remember (`line-editing` on emphasis).
- [ ] The full writing process (`business-writing`), not a one-pass draft.

## 7. Mission statements

A mission statement says why an organization exists, for whom, and what it commits to do,
so that employees, customers, and investors can pull in one direction. The rules:

- Get top management's approval of the approach before drafting.
- Prefer **definable, measurable, actionable** wording over slogans. "Be the best X in the
  world" fails; a statement of what you will do for whom passes.
- No jargon, buzzwords, platitudes, empty superlatives, or reflexive "quality".
- **Shorter is remembered.** A single sentence can do the job.

- Draft it with the people who must live by it, and test it: can a new hire use it to make
  a decision?

## 8. Finance additions (this skill's own)

Ethical promotion never overstates. In finance, overstatement is also a legal problem. Verify the current rule before relying on any of these.

- **Material news from a public company: Reg FD.** Material nonpublic information must reach
  the market broadly, not a favored few. The usual route is a widely distributed press
  release, often furnished or filed on **Form 8-K** (earnings under Item 2.02). Never brief
  an analyst or post the news first.
- **Forward-looking statements.** Releases that project results carry the PSLRA safe-harbor
  language: identify the statements as forward-looking and pair them with **meaningful,
  specific cautionary factors**, not boilerplate alone.
- **Testimonials and endorsements: the FTC Endorsement Guides.** Endorsements must reflect
  honest, actual experience; disclose any **material connection** (payment, free product,
  employment) clearly and conspicuously; don't present unusual results as typical. The FTC's
  2024 rule on consumer reviews bans fake or bought reviews.
- **Investment advisers: the SEC Marketing Rule (Rule 206(4)-1).** Testimonials are allowed
  with disclosures; performance shown must be fair and balanced, net of fees alongside gross,
  over standard periods (1, 5, and 10 years).
- **Broker-dealers: FINRA Rule 2210.** Communications must be fair and balanced, with no
  promissory or exaggerated claims; most retail communications need principal approval
  before use.
- **CAN-SPAM** (commercial email): no false header or deceptive subject line; identify the
  message as an ad; include a valid postal address; give a clear opt-out and honor it within
  10 business days.
- **Reading promotion as an investor.** A press release, investor deck, or annual-report
  narrative is promotional by design (`reports-and-proposals` on annual reports). Separate
  claims from evidence, look for the qualifier that was dropped, and check every number
  against the filed statements (10-K, 10-Q). Adjusted or non-GAAP figures must reconcile to
  GAAP.

## 9. Easy to confuse (standard practice)

- **Sales vs. informational brochure:** one asks for a purchase; the other educates and asks
  for nothing directly.
- **Organizational vs. subscription newsletter:** one serves a group it already has; the other
  must earn paying readers with information they can't get elsewhere.
- **Press release vs. newsletter article vs. memo:** a release is external news written for an
  editor (five W's, decreasing importance, boilerplate); a newsletter article tells a story to
  a known readership (lede, how, credit); a memo is internal and shorter (`memo-writing`).
- **Feature vs. benefit:** a feature is what the product has; a benefit is what the reader
  gets from it. Lead with the benefit.
- **Promotional vs. persuasive:** all promotion persuades, but a persuasive memo argues a
  decision to readers inside the organization; promotion builds demand or reputation with
  outsiders.
- **Endorsement vs. testimonial (FTC usage):** an endorsement is any message a consumer could
  take as someone's own opinion of the product; a testimonial is the customer version. Both
  need disclosed connections.

## 10. Before it goes out

- [ ] One purpose, one audience, and one main selling point or news point, stated early
- [ ] Benefits before features; the reader's side throughout
- [ ] Every claim true, qualified where needed, and provable; no knocking competitors
- [ ] Facts, names, numbers, and dates checked against the source
- [ ] Format conventions of the type (dateline and # # # for a release; masthead for a
      newsletter; action on the final brochure panel)
- [ ] Images licensed and credited (`research-and-sources` on copyright)
- [ ] A clear, easy next step for the reader, plus opt-out for any mailing
- [ ] Finance pieces checked against §8 before release
- [ ] Proofread (`grammar-and-mechanics`)
