# Research and Sources

Research is investigation in service of a purpose. It begins only after preparation
(audience, purpose, scope), and it ends in a document where every borrowed fact, idea,
quotation, and visual is credited. Documenting sources lets readers find and check them. It
also supports your argument, and it gives credit, which is what keeps the work clear of
plagiarism.

Sources: standard research practice, the APA's own free style pages (apastyle.apa.org), and
US Copyright Office and USPTO guidance (see ATTRIBUTION.md at the repo root). The
finance-source models and the "easy to confuse" list are this skill's own, labeled as such.

## When to Use

- Gathering material for a report, memo, proposal, or class paper
- Judging whether a source is good enough to rely on
- Quoting, paraphrasing, or citing anything, and building a reference list
- Planning an interview or a survey for a project
- Asking "is this plagiarism?" or "can I reuse this?"

**Where it sits in the family.** This skill is `business-writing` step 2. Run steps 1.1-1.6
(purpose, audience, scope, medium, premise, context gate) first; they decide what research
is needed. The premise check (step 1.5) verifies claims about the *relationship*; this skill
verifies claims about the *world*. For quotation punctuation, see `grammar-and-mechanics`.
For a formal report's reference section, see `reports-and-proposals`.

## 1. Plan the research

- **Start with what's known.** On the job, the main resources are your own knowledge and your
  colleagues'. For the assistant, that means the user's files and notes. Brainstorm which
  sources would serve the topic before searching.
- **Primary research** collects new data yourself: interviews, observation, surveys, focus
  groups, your own experience. Record it fully as you go (date, time, length) and interpret
  later. University research with human subjects may need ethics (IRB) approval.
- **Secondary research** uses material already analyzed and organized: books, articles,
  websites, recordings, podcasts, minutes, reports. Recent periodicals and .edu, .gov, and
  well-maintained corporate sites are good for current information. Wikipedia is a starting
  point: follow its references and check its edit date, and expect many readers not to
  accept it as a source.
- **Questions as a guide:** note the questions each source raises. The ones that stay
  unanswered direct the next round of research.

**Library strategy.** Start with a reference librarian. Match the source to the need:

- current government data → the web;
- a current scholarly article → a subscription database (e.g., Academic Search Premier,
  Expanded Academic, JSTOR, and LexisNexis, which is strong for business, legal, and
  financial material);
- an article older than the databases reach → a print index (Readers' Guide) or a
  newspaper's own archive;
- history → books and primary documents.

Search catalogs by subject or keyword and narrow with advanced search. Save results to a
citation manager (Zotero, RefWorks). Use reference works (subject encyclopedias,
specialized dictionaries, handbooks, bibliographies) for orientation.

**Web strategy:**

- Use precise keywords ("nuclear power", not "nuclear").
- Read the engine's search tips, try several engines, and refine as you go.
- Use Google Scholar for academic texts.
- A **metasearch engine** shows several engines' results at once; a **subject directory**
  (business → finance → investing) narrows to curated sites for selective research.
- No engine is complete or objective. Many don't index PDFs or databases.

## 2. Evaluate every source

Four criteria: **authority, accuracy, bias, currency.**

- **For any source:**
  - Is it current, relevant, and available?
  - Who is it written for?
  - Who wrote it, and are they an authority?
  - Does it document its sources so you can check them?
  - Is it objective, with opinions labeled and opposing views acknowledged?
  - Is the language appropriate and cogent?
- **Book:** the preface or introduction gives the purpose. The contents and index show
  relevance. Skim one relevant chapter.
- **Article:** a known publisher? The purpose, from the abstract or the headline and lead?
  Visuals that show its scope?
- **Website:**
  - Is the sponsor reputable, with a clear "About" page and disclaimers?
  - Is it current, with working links?
  - Can you cross-check its facts elsewhere?
  - Is it well designed and well written?

  Only a few domains are restricted (.edu, .gov), and a student can have a .edu address too.
  Be wary of wikis and unmoderated sites with no identifiable author.

**For finance (standard practice):**

- **Rank sources:** primary filings (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, proxy) and company-reported figures
  rank above data aggregators, which rank above press coverage, which ranks above opinion.
- **When figures disagree** across sources, cite the filing, and note the difference if it
  matters.
- **Date every market figure** (price, multiple, yield), because these go stale fast.

## 3. Take notes that can't turn into plagiarism

- **Let purpose and audience decide** what to extract. Record the key ideas and **every name,
  date, and definition** you'll need. When unsure whether to note something, weigh how hard
  the source would be to find again.
- **Paraphrase in your notes**, not word for word. Lifting a passage and changing a few words,
  then using it uncredited, is plagiarism even when it happens by accident.
- **Quote only the precise, striking, or authoritative**, and put quotation marks in the note
  itself, so you can tell later what was copied.
- **Record the full citation on first contact:** author, title, publisher, place, page, URL,
  and the dates of publication and access. Later notes from the same source need only the
  author and page.
- **Notes on ideas** must still make sense a week later. Don't compress concepts into
  nonsense.
- **Check each note against the original** before moving to the next source.

## 4. Interviews and questionnaires

**Interviewing for information:**

1. **Choose the person** whose expertise matches the purpose. Find them through colleagues
   or faculty, professional societies, organization "Contact" pages, or targeted searches.
2. **Prepare.** Research the person and their organization. When asking for the interview,
   say:
   - who you are and why you want to talk;
   - the subject and purpose;
   - the setting (in person, phone, video, or email);
   - roughly how long it will take.

   Ask permission to record, and offer them a chance to review the draft.
3. **Write specific, open-ended questions.** Not "Would the web help you?" but "Many
   physicians in your specialty answer routine questions online. How might that affect your
   relationship with patients?"
4. **Conduct it:**
   - Be on time, pleasant but purposeful.
   - Start easy and move toward the harder questions.
   - Let them talk; they're the expert. Stay objective, without debating.
   - Follow up on new threads, and redirect if the conversation wanders.
   - Take only memory-jogging notes, and don't let a recording make you skip questions.
   - Near the end, skim your notes and clarify anything ambiguous.
   - Ask whether you may follow up.
5. **Afterward:**
   - **Expand your notes immediately.**
   - Send a thank-you within a day or two.
   - Phone interviews lose nonverbal cues. Email interviews lose spontaneity: agree on the
     format first, say how many questions there will be, and ask for a deadline.

**Questionnaires:**

- **Trade-offs.**
  - *Pros:* reach more people, including people who are hard to reach; give respondents time
    to think; avoid interviewer influence; cost less.
  - *Cons:* skewed toward people with strong opinions; no follow-up questions; can be slower.
- **Sample:** only a **representative sample** supports a general claim.
- **Questions:**
  - Keep the survey short to raise the return rate.
  - Use easy-to-tabulate formats (yes/no, single or multiple choice), with few open-ended
    questions.
  - Word every question **neutrally**: "How satisfied are you with the new expense tool?"
    (with a scale), not "How much has the new expense tool improved your week?"
- **Cover message:** who you are, the purpose, the deadline, how to return it, and whether
  answers are confidential.
- **Form:**
  - A comments box.
  - Demographic questions only if they are essential.
  - Your contact information, and thanks.
  - Possibly an incentive, such as a copy of the results.

## 5. Quote, paraphrase, and credit

- **Paraphrase:** restate the essential idea in your own words and structure. It takes no
  quotation marks, but it **must be cited**.
- **Direct quotation:**
  - Quote word for word, in quotation marks, introduced with a comma or colon.
  - Use it sparingly, only when a source with expertise says something precise, striking, or
    reinforcing. Rarely quote more than a paragraph.
  - It must fit the surrounding sentence grammatically, introduced and commented on with
    transitions.
- **Indirect quotation:** a paraphrase, usually introduced by *that*, with no quotation marks.
- **Changes to a quotation:**
  - ". . ." marks words omitted mid-sentence; ". . . ." marks an omission at the end of a
    sentence.
  - No ellipsis is needed when you start mid-sentence (the lowercase letter signals it).
  - [Brackets] mark your insertions, and [*sic*] marks an error in the original.
  - **Never let an omission change the meaning.** That is unethical as well as inaccurate.
- **Long quotations:** APA style sets quotations of **40 words or more** as an indented
  block, with no quotation marks and the citation after the final period.

**Plagiarism** is using someone else's unique ideas without acknowledgment, or their exact
words without quotation marks and credit. Pasting web text into your work counts too. It
brings academic sanctions, legal action, and professional damage.

- **Common knowledge** needs no citation: information that is widely known and in standard
  references (the law of supply and demand), or generally known **within a field**. A sign:
  it appears in many sources without citation. **When in doubt, cite.**
- **In-house material** (manuals, reports, boilerplate) can be reused at work without
  citation; the company is the author. **In class, the same reuse without credit is
  plagiarism.**
- Material free to reuse (copyleft such as Wikipedia, Creative Commons, public domain) still
  needs credit.

## 6. Copyright, patents, trademarks

- **Copyright** protects original works from the moment they are created, with or without a
  © notice. To **reproduce or publish** copyrighted material, get permission from the
  copyright holder (the author or publisher, or per a website's terms of use). Say exactly
  what you want to use and how. Holders may charge a fee or set conditions.
  Being online doesn't make material free to use: altering or reusing web content usually
  needs the same permission.
- **Exceptions:**
  - **Fair use:** limited excerpts for teaching, commentary, criticism, news, or scholarship.
    It depends on all the circumstances; see copyright.gov.
  - **Company boilerplate.**
  - **Public domain:** U.S. government works, and works whose copyright has lapsed. Watch for
    separately copyrighted add-ons such as introductions, visuals, and indexes.
  - **Copyleft and Creative Commons** licenses.

  Credit is required even then.
- **Patents** give an inventor exclusive rights for a set term, in return for publishing
  the invention (USPTO):
  - **design**, 15 years from grant (for applications filed since May 13, 2015);
  - **plant** and **utility** (machines, processes, materials), generally 20 years from
    filing.

  "Patent Pending" means an application has been filed.
- **Trademarks** identify goods; **service marks** identify services. The USPTO also
  registers rarer kinds (certification and collective marks).
  - ® marks a registered mark; ™ and ℠ are used before registration.
  - Capitalize trademark names, and keep the ® when it is part of the name.

## 7. Document sources

**Which style:** the one your school, employer, or publisher specifies. Otherwise APA for
business and the social sciences. Read `references/citation-basics.md` for APA reference
models of common source types, finance-source models (10-K, earnings call, data terminal,
analyst report, your own interview), one source in APA, MLA, and Chicago side by side, and
links to each style's official free guidance.

**APA in the text (author-date):**

- **Signal phrase:** the author's name, a past or present-perfect verb, and the year in
  parentheses: *Okafor (2021) found that "..." (p. 9).*
- **Parenthetical citation:** *(Okafor, 2021)*. **Every direct quotation adds a page:**
  *(Okafor, 2021, p. 13)*.
- **Placement:** mid-sentence, after the closing quotation mark. For a block quotation, after
  the final period.
- **Two authors:** "and" in a signal phrase, "&" in parentheses: *Lee and Ortiz (2023)*
  / *(Lee & Ortiz, 2023)*.
- **Three or more authors:** first author + *et al.* in every citation.
- **Several works in one parenthesis:** alphabetical, separated by semicolons.

**APA reference list:**

- Alphabetical by author's last name, double-spaced, with hanging indent, on its own page.
- **Sentence case** for book and article titles (capitalize only the first word, the first word
  of a subtitle, and proper nouns).
- **Title case** for periodical names.
- **DOI as a URL** (https://doi.org/...) when one exists, otherwise the URL, with no period
  after either.
- *(n.d.)* when there is no date. A retrieval date only for content designed to change.

**Bibliography vs. references:** a reference list holds only what you cited. A bibliography
also holds works consulted for background. An **annotated bibliography** adds a short
description or evaluation after each entry. Alphabetize by author, or by the first
significant word of the title when there is no author (ignore *A*, *An*, *The*).

## 8. Easy to confuse

- **Primary vs. secondary research:** primary is data you gathered; secondary is what someone
  else already analyzed. A 10-K is a *primary source document* but *secondary data* for an
  analyst. Say which you mean.
- **Paraphrase vs. summary vs. quotation:** a paraphrase restates one passage at about its
  length. A summary condenses a whole work or section. A quotation is exact words. All three
  need a citation.
- **Common knowledge vs. a specific claim:** "Interest rates affect bond prices" is common
  knowledge. "The ten-year yield rose 40 basis points last quarter" needs a source.
- **Plagiarism vs. copyright infringement:** plagiarism is failing to credit; it's an ethics
  and academic offense even with permission. Infringement is reproducing without permission;
  it's a legal offense even with credit. Crediting a source doesn't give you permission to
  republish it.
- **References vs. bibliography:** what you cited vs. everything you consulted.
- **Fair use vs. public domain:** fair use is a limited, circumstance-dependent exception for
  copyrighted work. Public domain is uncopyrighted work.

## 9. Delivering

- Cite as you draft, not at the end. Every borrowed fact gets its citation when it enters the
  document.
- Never invent a source, a quotation, a page number, or a DOI. If a citation detail is
  missing, bracket it (`[page]`, `[access date]`) and say what is needed.
- When the user supplies sources, quote only what you have actually seen. When you
  paraphrase, say from which source.
- Name the citation style used, and flag any source that fails the four evaluation criteria.
