# Virtual Presentations

An in-person audience is more or less held in the room. An online audience isn't: other
tabs, the house, the inbox, and short attention spans all compete for them, and leaving
costs nothing. A virtual presentation keeps the rules of `presentations` but has to win
attention back all the time, through the camera, the sound, and regular interaction.

Sources: Mary Abbajay, "Best Practices for Virtual Presentations: 15 Expert Tips That Work
for Everyone," *Forbes*, April 20, 2020. Sections 2-6 follow the article's 15 tips, regrouped by stage.
The translation table (section 7), the recorded-talk adaptation (section 8), the
checklist timeline (section 9), and the "easy to confuse" list are this skill's own, not
the article's.

## When to Use

- A live presentation on Zoom, Teams, Webex, or a webinar platform, alone or as a group
- A recorded presentation, or a practice recording
- A setup or tech check before going on camera
- Planning a webinar's run-of-show: who shares, who watches chat, and when the audience
  takes part
- A reflection on what you personally need to adjust before presenting online

Load `presentations` first for the content: purpose, structure, slides, and rehearsal. This
skill covers what changes when the room is a screen. Hand any written piece, such as the
5.4 reply or a follow-up email, to `business-writing`; after the talk, `circling-back`
drafts the action-item email.

## 1. The premise: every presentation is a performance

The article closes on this point: in person or online, a presentation is a performance
done for the audience, and their time is valuable. Online you have to earn their attention
again and again, because nothing keeps them there.

## 2. Set the stage (tips 1, 2, 5, 6)

| Element | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Lighting** | Light from the **front**, bright on your face. Close the shades if a window is behind you. Natural light is best; if you present often and have none, buy a light |
| **Background** | Professional, on-message, and **uncluttered**. Find out whether the platform offers virtual backgrounds (Zoom) or blur (Teams). The background either adds to your presence or takes away from it |
| **Framing** | Show **face, neck, and shoulders**. Too far and you lose the face, which people are drawn to; too close and you're a floating head. Practice the distance |
| **Posture** | **Stand** if you can, with a standing desk or the laptop raised to eye level: it raises your energy and puts you in presenting mode. If you sit, **lean forward** like a news anchor. Never slouch back, which signals disconnection |

## 3. Own the technology (tips 3, 9, 10, 11)

- **Know the platform.** Fumbling with the tech kills a talk faster than anything. Do a
  **dry run** until its features are routine.
- **Rehearse on the exact setup** you'll use: the same computer, internet connection, and
  location. A rehearsal on a different setup doesn't test anything.
- **Get a co-host** (a producer or moderator) to run the technology, so you can focus on
  presenting.
- **Sound check** with someone on the other end. People forgive imperfect video, but they
  leave if they can't hear you. A headset or external microphone is sometimes better than
  the built-in one and sometimes not, so test it on that platform.
- **Plug into the modem** with an Ethernet cable for the strongest, most stable
  connection.
- **Build in redundancy:** give the co-host a copy of the slides so that, if your internet
  fails, they can share them while you dial in by phone.
- **Slides:** high-quality graphics and little text. The slides support what you say;
  they don't replace you.

## 4. Play to the camera (tips 4, 7, 8)

- **Look into the lens** when you speak, not at the screen or the faces. It takes
  practice, but each viewer feels you're looking at them. That is how eye contact works
  online.
- **Camera at eye level.** Too low gives you a double chin; too high and your gaze drops
  as you talk. Prop up the laptop.
- **Turn off self-view** if your own image distracts you.
- **On a panel or team, know when your camera is live.** If it's on while someone else
  speaks, look engaged.
- **Be animated.** A slow or monotone voice lets people tune out. Being engaging online
  takes deliberate energy.
- **Pace yourself without feedback.** You can't see the room's reactions, so don't speed
  up to sound energetic. Natural fast talkers slow down a little; slow talkers speed up a
  little.

## 5. Engage the audience (tips 12, 13)

- **Never go more than ~10 minutes** without audience interaction.
- Build in the platform's tools: **chat, polls, raised hands**.
- **Use names** from the participant list. Have people chat or raise a hand to speak,
  **keep a queue**, and call on them in order to unmute or turn on their cameras.
- **Let the co-host watch the chat.** Reading chat while you talk derails your train of
  thought.
- When you *ask* for chat responses, **stop talking and engage with them**: acknowledge,
  read some aloud, and respond.

## 6. Review and be yourself (tips 14, 15)

- **Record and play it back.** Note what worked as well as what to improve. Good
  presenters keep refining.
- **Be yourself.** Audiences connect with authenticity. Let your personality show and
  enjoy it: if you look like you're enjoying it, they will too. The article cites research
  that happy listeners retain more. **The audience takes its cue from your energy.**

## 7. How the in-person delivery rules translate

The delivery rules in `presentations` still apply online. This is how each one looks
on a screen:

| In-person rule | Online version |
| --- | --- |
| Eye contact builds rapport | Look into the lens; glance at faces only between points |
| Movement at transitions, but don't pace | Stand, or lean in at transitions; stay inside the frame |
| Gestures: keep one hand free | Gesture inside the frame, chest high, a little slower than in person |
| Touch, turn, talk at the screen | Point with the cursor or an annotation, pause, then speak |
| Projection: speak to the back row | The mic and sound check do the projecting; speak at a normal volume |
| Pace: not too fast, not too slow | The same, with no audience cues to help, so time it in the dry run |
| Backup printout and spare file | The co-host holds the slides, and you have the dial-in number |
| Phillips's slide rules (one message, six objects or fewer, no sentences) | The same, and they matter more on a small screen. Slides change often enough to hold eyes that could wander. A dark background still helps, because your video tile has to compete with the slide |
| Watch the audience's reactions | Watch the chat and reactions through the co-host, and plan interaction every ≤10 minutes |
| Beat anxiety by knowing the topic | Also by knowing the platform: rehearse on the real setup |

## 8. Recorded solo talks

Without a live audience, some tips change:

| Tip | Recorded version |
| --- | --- |
| Engage every 10 minutes | Ask rhetorical questions, pause for a beat, and move to a new visual or point at least every couple of minutes |
| Co-host and chat | Don't apply; you run the recording alone, so practice starting and stopping it |
| Redundancy | Save the file in two places, and record a short test clip first to check the audio |
| Record and review | Even more important: **watch a full take** before the final one and fix the two worst problems |
| Look into the lens | Keep it up for the whole recording; tape a marker beside the lens if your eyes drift to the slides |
| Pacing | Time the take against the assignment's limit |

## 9. Pre-session checklist

**The day before**
- [ ] Dry run on the same computer, connection, and room
- [ ] Co-host briefed: screen sharing, chat, polls, the speaking queue; they hold a copy of
      the slides
- [ ] Engagement points planned every ≤10 minutes (polls, chat prompts, named questions)
- [ ] Dial-in number saved, in case of an internet failure

**30 minutes before**
- [ ] Front light on and shades closed behind you
- [ ] Background uncluttered, or virtual or blur set
- [ ] Camera at eye level; framing shows face, neck, and shoulders; standing or leaning in
- [ ] Ethernet plugged in
- [ ] Sound check with someone on the other end
- [ ] Notifications off, other apps closed, the screen share tested with the right window
- [ ] Self-view off if it distracts you

**During**
- [ ] Look into the lens when speaking; look engaged when your camera is on and you're not
- [ ] Pause after asking for chat responses, and read some aloud

**After**
- [ ] Watch the recording: note strengths and fixes
- [ ] Send the follow-up email with action items (load `circling-back`)

## 10. Easy to confuse

| Pair | The difference |
| --- | --- |
| Screen vs. lens | Looking at faces on the screen reads as looking down or away; only the lens reads as eye contact |
| Presenter vs. co-host | The presenter delivers; the co-host runs the technology, watches chat, and holds the backup slides |
| Virtual vs. blurred background | Virtual replaces the room (Zoom); blur softens it (Teams). Either one has to support your image, not distract from it |
| Live vs. recorded | Live gets polls, chat, and named questions; recorded uses rhetorical questions, pauses, and frequent visual changes |
| Video quality vs. sound quality | Audiences forgive mediocre video but leave over bad audio, so put sound first |
| Energetic vs. fast | Animation comes from voice and expression, not speed; don't speed up to sound lively |

## 11. How to deliver help

- **Setup audit:** go through the section 9 checklist against the user's actual
  equipment and room, and list fixes in order of impact: sound, light, camera height,
  connection.
- **Run-of-show:** a timeline of the talk with the slide changes, an engagement point at
  least every 10 minutes, the co-host's duties, and the fallback plan.
- **Recording feedback:** use sections 4 and 8 first (lens, pacing, energy), then the
  `presentations` checklist for content.
- **A reflection on your own delivery:** a quick chat-style reply on what *you* will change. Pick the
  two or three tips that address your real weak spots, such as camera height or eye contact
  through the lens, and name the concrete fix. Hand it to `business-writing`, kept short.
